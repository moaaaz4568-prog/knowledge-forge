1. "Let me tell you about the last person who bought this."

In 2009 the Significant Objects experiment attached purpose-written fiction to thrift store junk and buyers paid 28 times the price, with the fiction fully disclosed. A true customer story is stronger than anything they invented. Open with one and the product stops being a spec sheet.

Specs describe. Stories price.

2. "Here's what was happening before they called us."

Every object in the experiment sold on a narrative, and every narrative starts in trouble. Your case studies do too. Tell the before honestly, the mess, the missed number, the tension, because a buyer who recognizes the before believes the after.

No before, no after, no sale.

3. "Picture the Monday after this goes live."

The experiment proved people pay for what an object means, not what it does. Meaning lives in scenes. Walk the buyer into one ordinary morning with the problem gone and let them stand in it for a second. A felt scene outsells a feature list every time.

Buyers pay for the scene, not the software.

4. "This product exists because of a customer we almost lost."

A one dollar vase became valuable the moment it had an origin. Products work the same way. Tell where yours came from, the failure that forced it, the customer who demanded it. Origin gives an offer a weight that no specification can add.

An origin story outsells a feature list.

5. "Their team doubted it too. Here's what changed their mind."

Proof works harder wearing a plot. The experiment's buyers did not bid on porcelain, they bid on what happened around it. Give your case study a beginning, a doubt, and a turn, and the same numbers you always quote suddenly carry.

Proof lands better wearing a plot.

6. "Here's the story the numbers are telling."

A dashboard is a pile of objects. The experiment says value arrives when someone arranges them into a narrative. Do not read the ROI table to the buyer. Tell them what it means: what is bleeding, what stops, what compounds. Same figures, different weight.

Numbers inform. Narrative moves.

7. "What's the story you'll tell about this a year from now?"

The experiment's last lesson: significance is assigned, not built in. Hand the buyer the pen for the ending. When they describe next year out loud, the purchase stops being your pitch and becomes their plot, and people do not abandon their own story.

Sell the memory before it happens.
