1. "Full honesty, there's one thing this doesn't do well. Let me tell you what it is."

Psychologist Elliot Aronson ran a study where a clearly talented person spilled coffee on himself, and people rated him more likable for it. The catch: it only worked because he'd already proven he was competent. Show your skill first, then admit one real flaw. A small, honest weakness makes everything else you said believable.

Competence first, then one honest flaw. That order sells.

2. "Here's the one type of person this is wrong for. If that's you, don't buy it."

A flawless pitch triggers suspicion. Nobody believes the thing with zero downsides. When you name a real limitation out loud, the buyer stops hunting for the catch, because you handed it to them. Volunteering the one weakness is what makes the toughen strengths finally land.

Name the flaw yourself and they stop looking for it.

3. "I'd rather undersell this and have you be surprised than oversell it and let you down."

Aronson's work showed people trust the person who doesn't oversell. Round your claims down, not up. Promise a little less than you know you can deliver, then beat it. The rep who underpromises and overdelivers builds the kind of trust no closing script can fake.

Underpromise on purpose. Let the result do the bragging.

4. "Honestly, I don't know. Let me find out and come back to you with the real answer."
Saying I don't know, once, is one of the most trust-building things a seller can do, because it proves everything else you said was true. A rep with an answer for everything sounds like a rep who's guessing. Admit the edge of what you know and the rest gains weight.
One honest 'I don't know' makes every 'yes' believable.

5. "I make money when you say yes. I want to be upfront about that."

Name your own incentive before the buyer suspects it. The moment you say the uncomfortable quiet part out loud, you take it off the table as a weapon they can use against you. Hidden motives get discovered and kill deals. Named motives build the trust that closes them.

Say your incentive out loud before they use it against you.

6. "I'm genuinely good at this part. I'm honestly not the right fit for that part."

The effect only works from competence, so lead with what you're excellent at, then admit where you're not. Confidence plus one honest limit reads as an expert. Confidence with no limits reads as a salesman. Pair your real strength with a real boundary and buyers relax.

An expert names a limit. A salesman pretends there isn't one.

7. "Before you decide, let me tell you the hardest part of this, the part most people struggle with."

Confess the difficult part first, unprompted. When you walk through the toughest thing before they hit it, two things happen: they trust you more, and they're prepared instead of blindsided. The rep who names the hard part upfront is the one still there after the sale.

Name the hard part first and you're the one they still trust later.
