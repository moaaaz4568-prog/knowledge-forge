Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

1. "Before I ask you anything, what made you take this call?"

Gong's 519,000-call discovery study found the winners spread their questions across the whole call. The average rep front-loads them, and the first five minutes turn into a form. Open with the one question that hands the buyer the wheel. Whatever they say first is the agenda. Leave your list alone until they've finished the sentence.

A buyer who sets the agenda answers everything else.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "Tell me more about that."

On the 326,000 calls, the reps who won asked 15 or 16 questions. The reps who lost asked about fewer, more strategic questions. Gong's read: 20. The most strategic one is the follow-up. When the buyer gives you a real answer, don't move to the next box. Stay there. Five words, then silence, and the second answer is the one you needed.

Checklists get answers. Follow-ups get the truth.
<!-- image 3 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

3. "What have you already tried?"

The same discovery data tied deal outcomes to one kind of question: the ones about the buyer's own problems, goals and what's in the way. Not the ones about your product. This gets the whole history in one shot: what failed, what it cost, who chose it. Let them tell it as a story. Don't interrupt it with your fix.

Every failed fix is a reason to buy yours.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. "I could be wrong. It sounds like the real issue is X. Is that fair?"

An interrogation is a run of questions the buyer never asked for. A check-back isn't a new question, it's proof you heard the last three, and Gong's advice on discovery is to keep it a two-way conversation. Say it with your own doubt in it. If you're right, they relax. If you're wrong, they correct you, and that correction is worth more than any question on your list.

Being corrected is the fastest way to be trusted.
<!-- image 5 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

5. "Two more from me, then I'll show you exactly how this works. Fair?"

Gong found the sweet spot on a discovery call is 11 to 14 targeted questions. Past that, the buyer stops feeling heard and starts feeling questioned. Count your questions. When you're at ten, tell the buyer where the questions end. The moment they know there's an end, they answer the last two properly.

Nobody minds a question that has an end in sight.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

6. "What would need to be true for this to be worth doing this quarter?"

The winners in the 326,000-call study talked less than the losers. The one way to talk less is to ask that carries three: the timeline, the budget and the decision, in the buyer's own words. Don't ask for a timeline. Don't ask for a budget. Ask this once, then write down every condition they name. That list is your proposal.

The buyer's conditions are the proposal. Yours is a guess.

7. "You've told me a lot. Want me to show you what I'd do with it?"

The turn. The winners asked few questions, then used the answers. The losers kept asking, because they hadn't listened well enough to know when to stop. Recap three things they said, in their words. Then present only the part of your offer that touches those three. Everything else can wait for the second call.

Discovery ends when the buyer hears himself in your pitch.

Now look at where all seven started. 326,000 calls, and the reps who won asked less and heard more.
