1. "Let me walk you through exactly how this works."

Marty's instinct under pressure was never to push harder. It was to slow everything down and explain, step by step, until the chaos in the room turned into a plan.

Panic makes a buyer feel like they're being rushed into a mistake. Clarity makes them feel like they're being handed a way out.

When the stakes spike, lower your pace. The calm explainer wins the room every time.

2. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm showing you the math."
Marty never begged anyone to believe him. He laid the numbers on the table and let them do the convincing.
People resist being persuaded but they can't argue with arithmetic they worked out themselves. Show the logic instead of asserting it.
Replace "trust me" with proof they can check, and the objection dissolves on its own.

3. "What happens to you if you don't do this?"

Marty kept whoever he was dealing with focused on their real alternative, which was almost always worse than the deal in front of them.

Buyers anchor on the cost of saying yes and forget the cost of standing still. Your job is to make the price of doing nothing impossible to ignore.

Make the status quo the scary option, not your offer.

4. "Give me the worst-case scenario. Let's look at it together."

Marty never ran from the objection. He walked straight into it and dismantled it out loud, in front of the person.

The fear a buyer won't say is the one that kills the deal in silence. Drag it into the open and it almost always shrinks.

Invite the worst case. Solving it on the spot is the most trust you'll ever build.

5. "I've already thought about the part you're worried about."

Marty stayed three moves ahead, so by the time someone raised a concern, he'd already built the answer.

Nothing reassures a nervous buyer like realizing you saw the risk before they did. It tells them you're not selling, you're protecting.

Anticipate the objection and answer it before they finish asking. That's what competence sounds like.

6. "We don't have to like it. We have to be smart about it."
Marty separated emotion from the decision, especially when the room was running hot.
A buyer caught in their feelings can't decide. Pull them up to the practical level and the choice gets simple again.
When emotion clouds the call, name it and step above it. Cool logic closes what heat can't.

7. "So here's what we do next."

Marty closed by removing the question entirely. He didn't ask if they were in, he calmly stated the next step as though it was already decided.

A confident, specific next move is easier to follow than a yes-or-no question is to answer. You're not pressuring, you're leading.

Stop asking for the decision. Start describing what happens now.
