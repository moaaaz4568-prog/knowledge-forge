1. He gave a free sample to earn a moment of attention.

Knocking doors with an armful of books, McConnell handed each housewife a small vial of rose perfume.

The gift bought him a moment to pitch, lowering the door's guard before he said a word about the product. A small giveaway up front earns the conversation that closes.

Give them a reason to let you in before you ask for the sale.

2. He listened to what the customer actually wanted.

McConnell noticed women were buying his books only to get the free perfume.

Instead of pushing harder on books, he followed the demand and started selling the perfume itself. The market was telling him what it wanted, and he paid enough attention to hear it.

Sell what they are reaching for, not what you walked in with.

3. He turned his best customers into his salesforce.

McConnell saw that the women buying from him would relate to other women better than he ever could.

So he recruited them to sell, starting with Mrs. Albee, who carried the line door to door and signed up more sellers behind her. The people who love the product make the most natural sellers of it.

Your happiest customer is your next best closer.

In 1886, when few women could earn their own income, McConnell offered them one.

That meant his sellers were motivated in a way salaried clerks never were, because every sale was their own money and their own independence. Tie effort to someone's own upside and they outwork everyone.

People sell hardest when the win is theirs to keep.

Within a year McConnell put his products into a printed catalog his reps could carry.

A beginner no longer needed his pitch or his presence, the catalog did the explaining and the rep just had to open the door. Give your people a tool that sells and you stop being the bottleneck.

Systematize the pitch so a rookie can run it day one.

McConnell named his company the Family Album and wrote to his reps personally.

Look after your people and they look after your numbers.
