1. "Forget the number for a second. What do you actually want?"

What set Steinberg apart was trust. Hardened, guarded athletes told him things they told no one else.

He called it peeling back the onion. He would stay in the room until the bravado dropped and the real hopes and fears came out, then negotiate for those instead of just the figure on the page.

Learn what they are truly buying and you stop guessing at the close.

2. "You don't need to be in the room for the ugly part."

One of his hardest rules was keeping clients and their families out of the actual negotiation.

He knew the other side would run a player down to his face to push the price lower, and that a proud athlete or a hot-tempered father could blow up a deal in one sentence. So he took the punches himself.

Carry the hard part for them, and a calm buyer stays a buyer.

3. "Here is the case for what you are worth. Every number, every comp."

When Steinberg landed Steve Young a 26.5 million dollar deal, the largest in the game at the time, he did not beg for it. He proved it.

He came in with the film, the stats, the comparable contracts, and the revenue his client would drive, until the number stopped sounding outrageous and started sounding obvious.

Build the case that well and your price feels like the only fair answer.

4. "Let's find the deal we both want to sign."

For all the record contracts, he refused to negotiate like a shark.

His reasoning was long-game. You might sit across from the same general manager for thirty years, so a deal that humiliates the other side quietly poisons every deal after it. He played mediator instead of enemy, and the deals kept coming for fifty years.

Win without leaving a corpse and they call you first next time.

5. "My word is worth more than any clause in here."

Here is the move almost no one copies. In exchange for those record contracts, Steinberg gave the teams something back.

His players would not hold out, would not demand a renegotiation a year later, would carry themselves like professionals. He made his own reputation the collateral, and teams paid up to deal with someone they could actually trust.

Your word, kept every time, becomes the thing that closes the next deal.

6. "This deal is the floor, not the ceiling."

He was the first agent to build his athletes into brands in their own right.

He pushed his clients into the communities and into business, partly because it was right and partly because it was shrewd. When kicker Rolf Benirschke gave to charity for every field goal, the city loved him, and a beloved player keeps his job far longer than a merely good one.

Sell the bigger future and one signature stops being the whole point.

7. "I'll be here long after this contract is signed."

He kept some clients for their entire careers, through injuries, lost seasons, and his own very public fall and comeback.

In a business where agents poach each other the moment a player slumps, he stayed. That loyalty is a big reason he landed the number one overall pick eight separate times across the decades.

Be the one who stays, and they bring you the next deal.
