1. "My job isn't to sell you something. It's to find the one that fits you."

Erica Feidner refused the word salesperson. She called herself a matchmaker, and Steinway's most guarded buyers dropped their defenses the second the role changed. Open your calls as the fit-finder and the buyer stops protecting their wallet and starts helping you help them.

Matchmakers get trusted. Salespeople get handled.

2. "Can I show you something you didn't come in for?"

The New Yorker wrote that her customers walked out with ambitions they never knew they had. She listened for the want underneath the stated need, then showed it to them. Your buyer asked for the small fix. Somewhere in that call is the bigger thing they actually want. Surface it.

They know their need. Show them their want.

3. "Here's the one I'd pick for you, and here's exactly why."
Inc. put her mechanism in plain print: once she was branded an expert, closing got easier. Experts don't present options and hope. They give a verdict. They diagnose and give one clear recommendation with your reasons attached, and the buyer follows it the way a patient follows a prescription.
Experts don't pitch. They prescribe.

4. "Honestly, you'd outgrow that one in six months."
Feidner matched people to where they were going. Buyers stretched their budgets for the right match and thanked her for it after. When a buyer reaches for the smaller option, tell them the truth about who it's too small for. That truth reads as care, and care closes.
Match the ambition, not the budget.

5. "Whether you buy today or not, you'll leave knowing exactly what's right for you."

Her rule was that the experience mattered whether they bought or not. She planted the seed and let it grow. Take the pressure out of the moment and give the full answer away. A relaxed buyer decides faster than a cornered one, and the ones who wait come back.

The pressure you remove comes back as trust.

6. "It's more than you planned to spend. And it's the only one that's actually you."

Stewart reported her buyers paying prices that strained their bank accounts, happily. Fit did that, not pressure. Never apologize for a number. Tie the price to the one option that genuinely matches their situation, and it stops competing with cheaper things that don't.

Nobody regrets paying for the right one.

7. "If the fit isn't right, I'd rather tell you than sell you."

Her whole model was the match, and a match means some buyers hear not this one. That's why every recommendation she made landed as truth. Show the buyer you'd rather lose the deal than force a bad fit, and your yes starts carrying real weight.

Willing to lose the sale is why they believe you.
