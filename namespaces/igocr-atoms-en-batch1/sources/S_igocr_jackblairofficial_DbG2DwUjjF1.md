1. "Do me a favor. Write your name down for me."

The napkin scene. In the film, nobody describes the pen. The winning move hands the man a reason to need one, and the sale finishes itself.

Stop opening with what the product is.
Open with the task, the moment, the gap it fills. Demand first, product second.

Create the gap. The product fills itself.

2. "When this breaks at 2am, who are you calling?"

Need feels theoretical until you put the buyer inside the moment of needing it. So build that moment out loud.

Walk them into the night the system fails, the quarter the pipeline dries up, the day the answer matters. Sell from inside that scene.

Sell the 2am moment, not the spec sheet.

3. "You don't need this today. The question is the day you will."
Honest demand has a date on it. This line concedes the present, which buys you the credibility to own the future.
No fake urgency and no pressure. Just move the conversation from whether they'll need it to when, and book the follow-up against that date.
Demand has a date. Find it.

4. "Try running next quarter without it. Walk me through how that goes."

The demonstration of absence. Instead of demoing the product, you demo the hole it leaves.

Let them narrate the workaround: the manual steps, the misses, the patchwork. Every sentence they say out loud is demand being spoken into existence.

Demonstrate the hole, not the plug.

5. "What broke, ran out, or got old this year? Start there."
The film's real lesson: you can't create demand for a man who needs nothing. You find the void that already exists.
Every buyer has something failing quietly. Inventory it before you pitch anything, and aim the whole conversation at that one spot.
Every sale starts at something empty.

6. "Forget the feature list. What's Tuesday morning worth to you?"

Supply and demand prices everything. The feature list is supply talk. The need is where the price actually lives.

Anchor the conversation to what the problem costs them in a normal week, and the number stops being about the product entirely.

Price the need, not the object.

7. "Put your name here, and the problem's mine now."

The close as a transfer of burden. Signature down, the thing they've been carrying moves off their desk and onto yours.

Said plainly, this reframes signing from spending money to setting something down. That's the version people say yes to.

The signature moves the problem to my desk.
