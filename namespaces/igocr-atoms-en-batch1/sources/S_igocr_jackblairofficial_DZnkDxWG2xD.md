1. "What would you do if you weren't afraid of getting it wrong?"

Wendy never argued someone out of their hesitation. She asked the question that walked them around it.

The buyer isn't stuck on your offer, they're stuck on the fear of making the wrong call in front of someone. Move the fear and the decision was never actually hard.

Stop answering objections. Start asking the question underneath them.

2. "You already know the answer. You're just waiting for permission to say it."

Half the people Wendy worked with had decided long before they admitted it. They just needed someone to make it safe to commit.

Most buyers aren't undecided, they're un-permitted. They want the thing and they're scared to want it out loud.

Give them the permission and you'll be amazed how fast the "maybe" was a yes.

3. "Say the real reason out loud. The polite one isn't slowing you down."

Wendy had zero patience for the surface excuse, because she knew the surface excuse never closed and never killed a deal.

"I need to think about it" is a door, not a wall. The real objection is behind it, and it's almost always smaller than the silence makes it feel.

Get the true reason on the table and you finally have something you can actually solve.

4. "Before you walk in there, fix your own state. They can smell it."

Wendy's first job was never the client. It was the person about to face the client. She regulated them first.

A buyer mirrors whatever you bring into the room. Walk in leaking doubt and they catch it. Walk in calm and certain and they borrow that instead.

Your own state is the first thing you sell, before a single word.

6. "I'm going to stop talking now."

Wendy understood that the most powerful thing in the room is the silence right after the real question.

The instinct to fill the quiet is what kills more closes than any objection. The person who speaks first to ease the tension almost always gives ground.

Ask. Then sit in it. Let them be the one to break the silence.

7. "This only works if it's your decision, not mine."

Wendy never claimed the win. She handed it back, because she knew a decision someone makes for themselves is the only one that holds.

People defend their own choices and resent yours. The close that sticks is the one the buyer believes was theirs all along.

Stop closing them. Start helping them. Start helping them close themselves.
