1. "That is a fair concern. Help me understand where it comes from specifically."

Hans Landa never attacked a position directly when a question would dismantle it more completely. He made the subject do the work of exposing their own reasoning.

This phrase invites the buyer to explain the objection in detail and detail is where weak objections fall apart. The buyer who has to articulate exactly why they are hesitating often discovers mid-sentence that the hesitation is thinner than it felt. You just gave the objection enough room to collapse under its own weight.

The objection that has to explain itself rarely survives the explanation.

2. "When you say it is not the right time, what specifically would need to change for the timing to work?"

Landa always made vague resistance concrete because concrete things can be addressed and vague things cannot.
This phrase converts the most common stall in sales into a specific set of conditions you can actually work with.

The buyer either names real conditions you can solve, or they realise in the process of naming them that none of them are as fixed as they assumed.
Both outcomes move the deal forward faster than any response you could have prepared.

Make the vague specific. Specific is always something you can close against.

3. "I am curious. What would it cost you if this problem is still unsolved in six months?"

Landa understood that the most powerful pressure is the kind the subject applies to themselves.
This phrase does not create urgency. It asks the buyer to calculate their own.

They do the maths out loud and the number they arrive at is always more persuasive than any figure you could have presented from your side of the table.
You asked one question. The buyer just closed themselves.

Let the buyer calculate the cost of inaction. They will always find a bigger number than you would have named.

4. "You mentioned that concern earlier and I want to make sure I understood it correctly. Can you say more?"

Landa catalogued everything and returned to it at exactly the right moment with surgical precision.
This phrase signals that you have been tracking the entire conversation at a level the buyer did not expect.

It also invites the buyer to expand on the objection, and expansion almost always introduces the contradiction that dissolves it.
The buyer who has to say the same thing twice rarely says it with the same conviction.
Conviction that fades on repetition was never the real objection.

Return to the objection when they least expect it. What comes back is always weaker than what went out.

5. "That is interesting. Every buyer I have spoken to in your position said something similar initially. Can I tell you what changed for them?"

Landa always used the broader pattern to reframe the individual position.
He made resistance feel like a familiar mistake rather than a unique and valid stance.

This phrase does not argue with the buyer. It places them inside a story that already has an ending. It places them in a pattern. The buyer who sees the pattern almost never repeats it.
They are no longer defending an original thought. They are deciding whether to repeat someone else's error.
That is a completely different psychological position and one that closes far more easily.

Put the objection in a pattern. The buyer who sees the pattern almost never repeats it.

6. "I want to make sure I understand your position completely before I say anything else. What is the core of it for you?"

Landa was never in a rush to respond when listening would give him more.
This phrase buys you complete intelligence before you spend a single word of your position.

The buyer who is asked to distil their objection to its core almost always reveals the one thing underneath everything else.
That one thing is the only objection you ever needed to address.
Everything else was noise you just had the patience not to chase.

Wait for the core. Everything around it is a distraction built to protect 🤝 CloserCoach.ai

7. "Given everything you have just told me, what do you think the right next step is?"

This is Landa's final move and the most devastating question in the sequence.
After a buyer has explained, expanded, calculated, and distilled their own position, asking them what comes next almost always produces the answer you needed.

They have been doing the thinking out loud for the last ten minutes.
You simply ask them to conclude it.
The buyer who writes their own next step never needs to be closed. The buyer who is already there.

Ask for the conclusion after they have done all the thinking. The close writes itself.
