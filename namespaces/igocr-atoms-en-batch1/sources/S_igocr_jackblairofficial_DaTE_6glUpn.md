1. "Tell me how it all happened."
Noesner's documented opener. Not
"what's your budget," not twenty
qualifying questions. One open door that
gets the whole story moving. His rule:
rapid-fire questions force one-word

2. "So what you're saying is... did I get that right?"
The FBI's negotiators paraphrase everything back. Not parroting, restating it in your own words so the person hears that you actually processed it. Noesner says being truly heard, sometimes for the first time in their life, is itself what changes people.
Feed it back in your words. Being heard moves people.

3. "I see. Go on."

The smallest tool in the FBI kit: brief, well-timed little replies. "OK." "I see." "Right." They feel like nothing, but they keep the other person talking, and Noesner's finding is that the longer they talk, the more control quietly passes to you.

Tiny words keep them talking. Talking hands you control.

4. When they blow up, go silent.
Noesner's pause. When emotion spikes,
most reps rush to defend, which feeds the
fire. Say nothing and something strange
happens: nobody can sustain a one-sided
argument. They burn out, calm down, and
come back to the table on their own.
An outburst can't survive your silence.
Wait it out.

5. "It sounds like the real issue isn't the price."

His cases turned on this: a man demanding his pension actually wanted his reputation back. The stated demand is rarely the real need. On your calls, "too expensive" is rarely about money. Find what it's actually about and answer that.

Answer the real need, not the stated demand.

6. "Before I suggest anything, let me understand."

The FBI stairway is strict: listening, then empathy, then trust, and only then influence. Skip a step and you're just pushing. Most reps pitch at step one. The phrase announces you've earned the ask before you make it.

You don't get to influence until they feel understood.

7. "Either way, it's your call."
Noesner calls it the paradox of power: the harder you push, the harder they push back. Handing the decision back does the opposite. Pressure creates resistance, freedom lowers it. The FBI doesn't corner people into yes, and neither do top closers.
Push and they dig in. Hand it back and they move.
