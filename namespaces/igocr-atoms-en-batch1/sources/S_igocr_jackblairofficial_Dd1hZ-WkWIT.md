1. "If the budget comes through, is this a yes? And if it doesn't?"

Students were told they had passed a big exam, or failed it, or that the grade was not in yet, then offered a cheap trip to Hawaii. Fifty four percent of the passes booked. Fifty percent of the fails booked. Only seven percent of the thirty two percent of the ones still waiting booked, though they would have gone either way. That is your buyer waiting on the budget. Ask them to decide under each outcome. If yes, the wait was never about the information.

A buyer who says yes both ways was never waiting.

2. "I'd rather have your no today than your maybe for a month."

The students who did not know their grade got a third choice: pay five dollars to decide after the grade came out. Many paid. They spent real money to delay a decision that came out the same way, because a reason attached feels safer than the same decision without one. A month of thinking is that five dollars, paid out of your pipeline. Name the cost of the delay out loud, then offer the fast no as the cheaper option.

Waiting is a purchase. Show them the receipt.

3. "Forget the other one. If it were only this, would you take it?"

A second study put a Sony player on a one day sale at $99. Sixty six percent of people bought it. Then the researchers added a top of the line Aiwa at $159 next to it. Sony sales fell to twenty seven percent, and forty six percent walked out with nothing. Two good options did not double the sales. They produced a no. When a buyer is stuck between your two packages, take one off the table and recommend the other by name.

Two good options is how a buyer buys nothing.

4. "Here's the version I wouldn't sell you, so you can see the difference."

When the second player was clearly worse than the Sonny, the walkouts fell to twenty four percent. A comparison the buyer can win makes the decision easy. So put the option you do next to the one you do, and say why it is wrong for them: the smaller tier, the cheaper competitor, the do it yourself route. You are not adding a choice. You are giving the choice a shape they can see.

A choice gets easy next to a worse one.

5. "I'm not going to add anything to the offer. Tell me what's missing from it."

Students were offered $1.50 or a nice pen instead. Seventy five percent took the pen. Offer the same pen or a second pen, and more than half kept the cash. Adding a sweetener made people keep their money. Reps do this to stalled deals every week: a bonus, a discount, an extra month, and the stall gets deeper, an extra closer. Stop adding. Ask what is missing, and fix that one thing.

Every bonus you add is another reason to wait.

6. "Can you get me an answer by Thursday, even if it's a no?"

The same researchers paid students five dollars to return a questionnaire. Given five days, sixty percent sent it back. Given three weeks, forty two percent. Given no deadline at all, twenty five percent. The more time people had, the less they did. A follow up with a no date is a follow up with no chance. Set a short date, make the no an allowed answer, and put it in both calendars before you hang up.

The more time you give a decision, the less it gets made.

7. "Between the two, which one would you keep?"

Asked which parent should get custody, sixty four percent picked the parent with the bigger positives and the bigger flaws. Asked which should be denied custody, most picked the same parent. Call, "which would you drop" sends the buyer hunting for flaws, and yours option, which they can see. Ask which they would keep, and they argue for the stronger option, which is yours.

The question you ask decides which facts they weigh.

Now look at every buyer you have marked "thinking about it". Not one is waiting for information.

Sound familiar?
