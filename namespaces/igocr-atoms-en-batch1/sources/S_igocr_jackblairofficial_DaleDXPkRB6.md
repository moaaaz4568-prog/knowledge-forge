1. "Before I pitch a thing, what's the part of this that's actually been a headache?"

Neil Rackham's team studied 35,000 real calls and found the reps who lose open by dumping features. The ones who win open by finding the problem. Features raise price resistance. Problems open the door. Kill the demo reflex and get them describing what's broken first.

Nobody buys the fix until they say the problem out loud.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blain
@jackblairofficial

2. "What's that actually costing you, past the obvious?"

This is the move that separated the top performers in the whole study. They asked roughly four times more of these than average reps. You make the buyer trace the real cost of the problem until it quietly outgrows your price. They name a small annoyance. You walk it downstream to the hours, the churn, the deals it kills.

A cheap problem gets ignored. Show them the full bill.
<!-- image 3 -->
Jack Blair

3. "Who else is stuck dealing with this when it goes wrong?"

Rackham found urgency doesn't come from pressure, it comes from spreading the problem across people and weeks. A problem that only bites your buyer waits forever. One that's hitting their team, their boss, and their customers gets a budget attached fast.

A problem with one victim waits. A problem with many gets funded.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial
4. "If this were handled, what would that actually change for you?"
The buyer describing the payoff in their own words beats you describing it, every single time in the data. They argue themselves into it. So stop listing what your thing does. Ask what solving it would mean, and let them build the case for you.
A benefit you claim is a pitch. A benefit they say is a reason.
<!-- image 5 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

5. "I'll stop talking. What's your honest read so far?"

The study buried the hard close. In big deals, aggressive closing didn't just fail, it backfired and raised the buyer's guard. The winners talked less and let the buyer fill the space. When you feel the urge to push, hand them the silence instead. What they say next is the real state of the deal.

On a real deal, the one who talks least holds the most.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair

6. "I did my homework, so I'll skip the basics. Here's the one thing I couldn't figure out on my own."

Rackham found fact-gathering questions bore buyers and quietly lower your status. The best reps ask fewer, sharper ones and walk in already knowing the easy answers. Never spend a live call collecting what a search bar could have told you.

Every question you could've googled costs you rank.

7. "What makes fixing this now better than fixing it later?"
The strongest questions in the study pulled the buyer into stating their own urgency. The top closers never argued the deadline. They asked why now beats later, then went quiet and let the buyer talk themselves out of "let me think about it."
Urgency you push is resistance. Urgency they say is a decision.
