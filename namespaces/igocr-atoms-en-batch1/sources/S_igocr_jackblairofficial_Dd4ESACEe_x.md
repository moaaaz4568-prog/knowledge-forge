1. "My name is Dana. I've been a nurse on this floor for eleven years, and I'll be with you until seven."

Hospitals teach it as AIDET, a Studer Group script used across American hospitals: acknowledge the person, introduce yourself with your name, your skill and your experience, then say how long you'll be there. The credential comes before the care. On a call, your years and your lane are the first thing out of your mouth, not the last.

Trust starts with who you are and how long you'll be around.

2. "This will take about ten minutes."
Duration is the D in the same script, and
the reason is anxiety. A patient who knows
how long the needle takes stops bracing.
A buyer who knows the call is fifteen
minutes and the decision comes at the
end stops looking for the exit. Say the
length, say when the ask comes, then
keep to it.
A known length is easier to say yes to than
an open one.

3. "Here's what I'm going to do, and here's why."

Explanation is the E. Nurses narrate the next step before they take it, in plain words, because a step you saw coming doesn't hurt the way a surprise does. Reps do the opposite. They hold the price and the process back and call it strategy. Tell the buyer what happens next before it happens, including the part where you ask.

Nobody flinches at a step they saw coming.

4. "What matters most to you today?"

In 2012 two doctors wrote in the New England Journal of Medicine that the question should change from "what is the matter with you" to "what matters to you". It became a movement in hospitals around the world. It is also the only discovery question you need, because it replaces your feature list with the buyer's own reason.

You can't sell what matters until they say it out loud.

5. "So I know I explained it well, tell me how you'll take these at home."

Teach back, from the federal AHRQ toolkit. The nurse doesn't ask "any questions?" She asks the patient to say the plan back in their own words. On one rehabilitation unit it cut thirty day readmissions by 45 percent. The buyer who repeats the plan back in their own words has bought it twice, once from you and once from themselves.

What they can say back, they keep.

6. "This is Maria. She's got you tonight. I've told her everything, so you won't have to repeat yourself."

The warm handoff, done at the bedside, in front of the patient. The next person is introduced by name, the story travels with them, and the patient never starts over. Reps lose more deals in the handoff to onboarding, finance or the manager than they lose in the close. Do it live, by name, with the story intact.

A handoff made in front of the buyer is a promise kept in public.

7. "Is there anything else I can do for you before I go? I have the time. I'll be back at three."

The scripted close of hourly rounding. In a six week study across 27 units in 14 hospitals, that visit, ending with an open question and a stated return time, cut call lights, cut falls and raised satisfaction. The open question says you're not rushing out. The time says you're coming back.

End every call with the open question and the hour you'll call again.

Now be honest with yourself. You already run half of these on every call. What is the room paying you for it?

Sound familiar?
