MADAM C.J. WALKER WENT DOOR TO DOOR WITH $1.50. TWELVE YEARS LATER SHE HAD TRAINED 20,000 WOMEN AND WAS AMERICA'S FIRST SELF-MADE FEMALE MILLIONAIRE. HERE ARE THE 7 PHRASES THAT BUILT IT:

1. "Before I say a word, look at my hair. Two years ago it was falling out in my hands."

Denver, 1905. A laundress with $1.50, a jar of hair grower she mixed herself, and a scalp that had been going bald. She sold door to door and the proof came first: her own head, before and after, later printed in every ad she ran. Nobody had to believe a promise. They had to look. Open with the result you can show, not the product you want to describe.

Proof you can see beats a promise they have to trust.
<!-- image 2 -->

2. "I'm not here to sell you a jar. I'm here to show you what to do with it."

She called it the Walker System: wash, treat the scalp, comb, repeat, and she taught it in kitchens before she ever had a school. By 1908 the school existed, Lelia College in Pittsburgh, turning customers into trained hair culturists. The jar was the ticket. The method was the sale. Sell the way you'd use it and the product rides along.

People buy the hands, then the jar.

3. "Who on this street has hair like you'd want? Take me to her."
Every demonstration ended in a home, and every home had a neighbour. Her agents worked streets, churches and clubs the way a good rep works a referral: the woman who just said yes points you to the woman who will. The company who grew the territory grows, one door at a time, from people who had already seen it work.
The best lead is the neighbour who already believes.

4. "This has nothing to do with vanity. Your scalp is a health problem, and I can fix it."

Churches called hair products vanity and worse. Walker refused the word straightener for what she sold and talked about hygiene, scalp health and hair that stopped falling out. Same jar, different problem. When a buyer's objection is moral or personal, move the product from the want column to the need column and the argument changes shape.

Nobody argues with a health problem.

5. "I came from the cotton fields. I was promoted to the washtub. Then I promoted myself."

Chicago, 1912. The National Negro Business League would not put her on the program, so on the last day she stood up from the floor and said it anyway: from the cotton fields to the washtub to the cook kitchen to a factory on her own ground. When nobody introduces you, introduce yourself once, with your record, and sit down.

A credential is what you say when nobody says it for you.

6. "Sell the most this year and there's a prize. Give the most to your church and there's a bigger one."

Philadelphia, 1917. Two hundred agents at the first national convention of her sales force, and the prizes went two ways: the women who had sold the most, and the women who had given the most to charity. She made the yes visible, then made the reason for it visible. Status in a room of peers moved more product than any commission table.

People sell harder for a room that will see it.

7. "You could keep washing other people's clothes. Or you could sell this and never do it again."

Her recruiting pitch was her own life, said back to women who were still living it. Laundresses, cooks and maids became agents on commission, with a trained skill and a territory of their own. The offer named the life the buyer was leaving before it named anything else.

The strongest pitch names what they get to stop doing.

Now be honest with yourself. You already run half of these on every call. What is it paying you for?

Sound familiar? What is it?
