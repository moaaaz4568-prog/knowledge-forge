3. Sell the gap, not the thing.

Every Mays pitch had two pictures. Dirty rug, clean rug. Greasy oven, spotless oven.

He never sold the product. He sold the distance between where the buyer was and where they wanted to be.

Whoever paints the after-picture wins the room.

4. Stack the offer after the yes.

"But wait, there's more!" wasn't a gimmick.
It was timing.

Mays let them say yes first. Then he
stacked a second bottle, a guarantee, a
price drop on top.

Every additional yes feels lighter than the
first one. Stack while the door is open.

5. Remove "later" from the table.

"Call in the next 20 minutes" wasn't filler. It was the only line standing between the buyer and the easiest answer of all: I'll think about it.

The buyer who walks away rarely comes back. A deadline is the cheapest way to kill "later".

6. Let someone else say it.

Every Mays pitch ended with somebody who wasn't him. A customer testimonial. A guarantee. A tougher stain than any customer would face.

He'd build belief, then hand it to a voice that wasn't his own.

The voice the buyer trusts most is the one with nothing to gain.

7. Plant the high number first.

"$39.99 in stores. But right now, two bottles for $19.95."

The number they hear first is the one they brace for. Mays planted $39.99, then made $19.95 feel like winning.

Anchor high so the real price feels like relief.
