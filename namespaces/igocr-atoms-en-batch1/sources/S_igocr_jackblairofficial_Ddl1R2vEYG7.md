1. "I'm not going to argue the part you're right about."

In 1924 Darrow took the Leopold and Loeb case with two confessed killers and a city that wanted them hanged. He did not fight the facts. He pleaded them guilty, which moved the case away from a jury and put one question in front of one judge. Stop defending the part your buyer is right about. Then name the one question the deal turns on.

You cannot win the argument you should not be having.

2. "Before I tell you anything, tell me how you got here."

Darrow wrote that almost every case is won or lost when the jury is sworn. He picked jurors on temperament, not on logic, and he studied who was in the chair long before he decided what he would say. The first four minutes of a call are for finding out who you are actually talking to, and what they had already decided before you dialled.

You cannot move somebody you have not read.

3. "Let me say that in plain English."

Darrow refused the courtroom voice. He spoke to farmers and mechanics the way they spoke to each other, and he treated a juror who could not follow him as his own failure, never theirs. Your buyer will never tell you they did not understand. They will tell you they need to think about it.

Confusion never signs anything.

4. "You're the one who has to live with this. Not me."

At the Leopold and Loeb sentencing Darrow spoke for roughly twelve hours to a single judge, John Caverly, and told him the responsibility was his alone. He never begged. He handed the weight over and let it sit there. When your buyer hesitates, stop pushing. Hand them the decision out loud, then stop talking.

Pressure makes people stall. Ownership makes them decide.

5. "What would you have done, standing in that house?"

In 1926 Darrow defended Henry Sweet, a Black man who fired from a Detroit house surrounded by a white mob, and he asked an all white jury to stand inside that house for a few minutes. They acquitted him. When your buyer is on the fence, stop listing features and put them inside the Monday after they say yes.

Nobody buys a description. They buy a place they can stand.

6. "I'm not going to tell you the other guy is wrong."

In Boise in 1907 Darrow defended Big Bill Haywood on a murder charge and never once tried to make the prosecution look dishonest. He made their case look thin instead, and Haywood walked out acquitted. When your buyer names a competitor, do not run them down. Name the one thing that setup cannot do.

Go after the case, never the man. The room is watching how you treat him.

7. "Let me say the worst thing about this before you do."

Darrow said the ugly part out loud first. In 1924 he stood up and called what his clients did exactly what it was, in the first minutes, before the state could do it for him. Once he had said it, nobody could use it on him. Say your price and your one real weakness before your buyer finds them.

Now look at what all seven of those actually are. Not one of them is information. Every one is a live human reading another one.

Sound familiar?
