02 / 10
"I'LL GO LAST. WHAT'S YOUR READ FIRST?"
Roger Shuy helped build forensic linguistics in the US by taking apart recorded conversations for courts. One thing his cases keep showing is that whoever speaks first sets the frame, and everything after gets heard through it.
Hold your position and take theirs raw. You only learn what someone thinks before you tell them what to think.
Speak second and you find out what they actually believe.

03 / 10
"WAS THAT A YES, OR WAS THAT JUST POLITE?"
In tape cases, feedback markers get scored as consent. Uh huh and okay mean somebody is following along. They are not the same as somebody agreeing.
Ask it directly when you hear one. It costs you four seconds and saves you a month of follow up.
Uh huh means I hear you. It has never meant I agree.

04 / 10
"SAY IT BACK TO ME. I WANT TO KNOW IF I EXPLAINED IT BADLY."
Shuy's work turns on the gap between what a speaker meant and what a listener recorded. The listener fills every ambiguity with the meaning he already expected.
Make them restate it in their own words. Put the fault on your explanation so restating costs them nothing.
What they cannot repeat, they never agreed to.

"TELL ME THE PART YOU'RE NOT SOLD ON YET."
His transcripts show people rarely refuse outright.
They go quiet on the one piece they reject, and the
silence gets read as consent for the whole thing.
Name that you expect one, then wait. The gap is
where the real objection lives.
Silence on one point is not agreement on all of them.

07 / 10

"WHICH PART OF THIS WOULD YOU DEFEND IF SOMEONE PUSHED BACK?"

Courts look for whether a speaker ever committed in his own voice. Commitment shows up when a person argues for something, not when they let it pass.

Ask them to defend one piece to an imaginary sceptic. Watch which piece they pick.

People only defend what they actually decided.

"PUT IT IN WRITING IN YOUR WORDS, NOT MINE."
This whole discipline exists because spoken agreement is unreliable evidence. Self authored language is a different order of proof, in a courtroom and on a deal.
Ask for one line from them. Not a signature, a sentence.
A yes in their handwriting is the only that holds.
