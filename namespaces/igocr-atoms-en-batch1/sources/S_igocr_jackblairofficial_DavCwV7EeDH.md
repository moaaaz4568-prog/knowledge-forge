1. "Before we talk business, I did my homework on you. Not your company. You."

In 1947, no major corporation would advertise in a Black magazine. Ebony founder John H. Johnson wanted Zenith, so he studied its chairman and found the man's obsession: he'd trekked toward the North Pole, and his hero was Matthew Henson, the Black explorer who got there with Peary.

Johnson tracked Henson down before the meeting.

Study the person. The company signs later.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial
2. "You said no pitch. Fine. Ten minutes, and I won't pitch. I promise."
Zenith's chairman took the meeting on one condition. It would not be about advertising in Ebony. Johnson agreed, walked in, and never broke the rule. He let the room do the selling instead.
A buyer who's written you off will still give you ten minutes if the ten minutes cost them nothing.
Take the meeting on their terms. Win it on yours.

@jackblairofficial

3. "That's not a promise. It's already done. Look."

Johnson didn't tell the chairman Ebony could cover him like Henson. He'd already run a four-page Henson feature in that month's issue, and he'd brought a copy of Henson's book, signed.

As Johnson told it, the chairman pointed at the snowshoes on his own wall and asked why no magazine covered Matt Henson. Johnson laid both on the desk.

Done beats promised in every room on earth.

4. "Forget doing me a favor. You're leaving money on the table."

Johnson never sold sympathy. He sold an untapped market with numbers attached, to men who thought they were being asked for charity. Zenith signed, then Swift, Elgin, Armour, and Quaker Oats followed.

Buyers move for their own interest and only their own interest. Aim every sentence at theirs.

Never sell sympathy when you can sell profit.

5. "Audit everything. I'll wait."

Ebony became the first Black magazine tracked by the Audit Bureau of Circulations. Johnson volunteered for the scrutiny nobody demanded, because verified numbers end arguments that promises can't.

When a buyer doubts you, don't defend. Hand them the tools to check you and sit still while they do.

Volunteer the audit before they ask for it.

6. "Nobody would go first. So we went first on ourselves."

When no advertiser would touch the magazine, Johnson built his own mail-order companies and filled the ad pages himself, proving the pages could sell product.

If no one will be your first case study, become it. A skeptical buyer doesn't need your confidence. They need one working example.

Be your own first case study.

7. "I'm not asking you to change how you work. I'm asking for a seat inside it."

Johnson said it plainly: I don't want to destroy the system, I want to get into it.

Buyers who feel threatened dig in. Buyers offered a low-risk seat at their own table lean forward. Kill the invasion frame before it forms, and the wall you were fighting becomes a door.

Enter as an ally, not an invasion.

Read those back. Not one of them asks for a chance. They remove the buyer's reasons for dismissing you, one piece of homework at a time, which is how one man sold his way through doors that were locked on purpose.
