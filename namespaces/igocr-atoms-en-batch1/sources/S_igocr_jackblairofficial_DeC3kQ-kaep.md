1. "You're probably right to be skeptical. Most people who call you waste your time."

South Omaha, 1910. Before the book that sold 30 million copies, Dale Carnegie sold bacon and soap for Armour and made his territory the company's national leader. His first rule of winning people over: the only way to get the best of an argument is to avoid it. Open by agreeing with the buyer's guard. They came braced for a pitch. Give them the point before they make it and the fight they prepared for never starts.

Agreement disarms faster than any proof.

2. "Fair to say you'd want this handled before the busy season either way?"

His rule: start with questions to which the other person will answer yes. Three questions they can only agree with before the one that costs them something. Timing, problem, outcome. The real question lands on a roll, not on a wall.

Nobody says no to their own fourth yes.

3. "Walk me through what's happening now. I'll shut up."

His rule: let the other person do a great deal of the talking. People talk themselves into a decision faster than they can be pushed into one. Ask it and go quiet. Every minute they talk hands you the objection, the budget and the decision maker for free.

The buyer who talks is selling. To themselves.

4. "That sounds like your idea more than mine. Did I get that right?"
The rule: let the other person feel the idea is theirs. Take the deadline, the budget or the feature they mentioned and hand it back as theirs. Defending the plan now means defending themselves.
Nobody argues with their own idea.

5. "Dana, what would you need to see to be sure?"

His rule on names: a person's name is, to that person, the sweetest and most important sound in any language. Use it at the open, once in the middle, and on the question that asks for the decision. Never in every sentence, or it reads as a script.

A question with their name in it gets an honest answer.

6. "Every month this waits is about $2,400 you don't get back. Want me to show you where that comes from?"

His rule: dramatize your ideas. Truth alone isn't enough, it has to be made vivid. Don't say the cost of waiting. Show it as a number they can picture and offer to prove it.

A number they can picture beats an argument they can debate.

His last rule of the twelve: throw down a challenge. Say it once, then stop talking. The silence does the work.
The challenge only works if you can stand the quiet after it.
