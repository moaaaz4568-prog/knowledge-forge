1. "This call isn't about me. It's about where you're trying to get."

Donald Miller's whole framework rests on one line: the customer is the hero, and you're the guide. Brands that cast themselves as the hero lose. The second you stop selling yourself and start mapping their goal, the buyer leans in, because now the story is about them.

Make the buyer the hero. You're the guide.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "I get why this has felt stuck. I've walked people out of the exact same spot."

A guide earns trust with two things and only two: empathy and authority. One sentence proving you understand the frustration, one proving you've solved it before. Not your resume. Buyers don't follow the loudest person in the room. They follow the one who's clearly been where they're going.

Empathy proves you care. Proof shows you can.
<!-- image 3 -->

3. "The lost deals aren't the real problem. It's what they're doing to your confidence."

People can name their external problem, but they buy the solution to the internal one. Miller's point is that the surface issue is never what actually moves them. Name the feeling underneath the fact, and the buyer feels understood in a way no competitor bothered to try.

People buy the fix for the feeling, not the fact.
<!-- image 4 -->

4. "Here's the whole path. Three steps, that's it."
Customers trust a guide who hands them a plan. Confusion is what kills the deal, so a short, named path removes the fear of the unknown. Three clear steps beat an impressive but foggy pitch every single time, because the buyer can finally see themselves getting to the other side.
A simple plan beats an impressive pitch.
<!-- image 5 -->

5. "So here's the one thing to do next."
Miller is blunt that people don't act unless you directly call them to. Most reps hint, soften, and bury the ask under five options. Name one clear next step out loud, then stop talking. A buried invitation is the same as no invitation at all.
Nobody acts on a hint. Ask straight.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair

6. "If today's a no, here's the smaller step that keeps it moving."
The framework gives you two asks: the direct one and the transitional one. When the big yes isn't there yet, offer the small commitment that keeps them walking forward instead of away. A resource, a trial, a next call. Never end a conversation on nothing.
When the big yes isn't ready, offer the small one.

7. "Say it back to me. If it's not clear, that's on my explaining."

If you confuse, you lose. Miller's test is simple: a stranger should grasp what you do and what to do next in seconds. When a buyer can repeat your offer back in one plain sentence, they can sell it to themselves, and to whoever else has to sign off.

A confused buyer never buys. Clarity closes.
