1. He led with value before he ever asked for the sale.

Burg built everything on one law: your worth is how much more you give than you take.

He front-loads help, insight, and introductions long before any ask, so by the time he sells, the relationship already owes him nothing and trusts him completely. Value first makes the sale a formality.

Give something real before you pitch anything.

2. He turned customers into an army of referrers.

Burg stopped chasing one sale at a time.

He built a network of people who knew, liked, and trusted him, what he called personal walking ambassadors, who sent him business whether or not they ever bought. One happy contact becomes a pipeline.

Turn every good relationship into a source of introductions.

3. He sold on value, never on price.

Burg saw that the second you compete on price, you become a commodity.

So he moved every conversation on to the value and outcome instead of the number, because price is what they pay and value is what they get. Cheapest is a race you lose even when you win.

Anchor the talk on what it's worth, not what it costs.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. He stopped keeping score.

Most people help others expecting a favor back.

Burg gave without tallying who owed him, because the moment people sense you're keeping score, the trust evaporates. Genuine giving compounds; transactional giving stalls.

Help people without tracking what you're owed and the returns find you.

5. He put the other person's interest first.

Burg measured influence by how much you put others ahead of yourself.

When the buyer can feel that you're genuinely steering toward their win, not yours, resistance drops and they hand you the deal. People buy from those who are clearly on their side.

Make their outcome the obvious center of the conversation.

6. He sold by being himself.

Burg taught that the most valuable thing you bring is you.

Polished scripts and borrowed personas leak, but authenticity is the one thing no competitor can copy, so he stopped performing and started showing up real. People trust a person, not an act.

Drop the sales voice and let them buy from the actual you.

7. He stayed open to receiving.

Burg's givers had one last discipline: let the good come back.

Giving constantly without ever allowing yourself to receive breaks the cycle, so he accepted the referrals, the deals, and the help without guilt. The exchange only works if both sides flow.

Give freely, then actually accept what comes back to you.
