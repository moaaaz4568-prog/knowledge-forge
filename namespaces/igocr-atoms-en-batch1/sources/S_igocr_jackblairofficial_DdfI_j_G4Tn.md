1. "That was one call. It's not a verdict."

Martin Seligman tested MetLife's agents on how they explained a bad day. The ones who read it as a no as permanent ("I always lose these") quit more and sold less. The ones who read it as temporary kept dialing and outsold them. Say the sentence out loud the second the call ends, before the next dial. Name the buyer, the day and the objection. Anything you can name is smaller than anything you can't.

Permanent is a story. Today is a fact.

2. "He said no to the price. He didn't say no to me."

The second thing Seligman measured was reach. Pessimists let one no spread into everything (I'm bad at this). Optimists kept it in the box it came in. Write the objection down word for word. If you can't quote it, you've already turned it into a judgment about yourself, and that's the version that follows you into the next call.

A no you can't quote can't grow

3. "What's the evidence for that?"

Seligman's tool for it is disputation: catch the belief, then cross-examine it the way a lawyer would, with evidence, not with cheer. It's the D in his ABCDE method. Put last month's closes next to this afternoon's no. Count the ones that closes to the count doesn't get to run the next call.

Your pipeline is the evidence. Your mood is not.

4. "Fair enough. What would have made it a yes?"

Optimists in Seligman's data treated a setback as information about the situation, not about themselves. Information you can act on. A verdict you can only carry. Ask it before you hang up, in that tone. Whatever they answer is the opening line of your next call with them, and the fix for the next ten calls with everyone else.

A no with a reason is a lead.

5. "Next call. Now, while it still stings."

The special force at MetLife, the reps who failed the screen, outsold the pessimists by 21% in year one. By Seligman's account the pessimists also quit at twice the rate. The difference was persistence after the no, not talent before it. Dial inside ten minutes. The last call hardens with every minute you give it, and the next buyer can hear it in your voice.

Rejection has a half-life. Beat it to the phone.

6. "Here's what I'd change, and here's what I'd keep."

Seligman was careful about this. It isn't denial. He called it flexible optimism, and told people to run the pessimist's check when the cost of being wrong is high. Learn the lesson, then drop the verdict. One sentence on what you'd change. One on what worked. Then close the file. A review with twenty becomes the story you tell about yourself.

Blame nothing. Fix one thing. Fix one thing.

7. "Judge me on the year, not the afternoon."

The 57% gap showed up in year two, not year one. The optimists were still there, still dialing, and the numbers had time to compound. Keep one count: calls made in the hour after. Not closes, not quota. That number predicts your year better than any afternoon does.

The gap shows up in year two. Be there for it.

Now look at where all seven started. 129 reps who failed the state screen, a psychologist with a questionnaire, and a year two that went their way.
