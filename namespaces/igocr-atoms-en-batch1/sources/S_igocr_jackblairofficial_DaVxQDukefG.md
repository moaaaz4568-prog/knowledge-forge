1. He stopped asking how do I compete with the big agencies.

That was the wrong question, and he knew it was sinking him. It kept him meeting about his disadvantages. His size, his resources, his disadvantages.

The moment he threw it out, he had room to ask a better one.

Before your next call, catch the question you're secretly prepping around. If it's about you, it's the wrong one.

2. He sat in the buyer's chair on paper.

He wrote down every question he would have if he were the client. Who is this guy? Can he deliver? What happens after he has my money?

He got to fifty questions, then cut it to the ten that mattered most.

Write the buyer's real questions before every call, not the ones you wish they would ask.

3. He answered the hard ones before they were asked.

Not the easy softballs. The uncomfortable ones the client was privately worried about but might never say out loud.

A concern you name for them lands as honesty. The same concern left for them to raise lands as a red flag.

Find the objection they are too polite to say, and say it first.

4. He opened by handing over the list.

He did not launch into a pitch. He said he had put himself in the client's position and written down what they would want to know, then walked through it.

The client had made his own list of ten questions. Duffy had already answered them all.

Lead with their agenda, not yours. Show them you did the work from their side.

6. He made preparation his edge, not his budget.
