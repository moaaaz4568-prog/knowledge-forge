1. "I'm going to check in every month until this is a yes or you tell me to stop. Both work."

In 1970, a promoter named Jerry Weintraub started calling Elvis's manager, Colonel Parker, every single morning with the same sentence. I want to take Elvis on tour. The way Weintraub told it, the answer was no for a solid year. Then one morning it wasn't.

Persistence that announces its own schedule stops being pestering and becomes positioning.

persistence reads as conviction, not desperation.

2. "You're the account I want this year. I'm not hiding that."

Weintraub wasn't working a list. He'd written one sentence down after a dream: Jerry Weintraub Presents Elvis Presley. One named target, chosen out loud.

That's the opposite of chasing ghosts. A pipeline of accidental maybes drains you. One declared might whale focuses you, and buyers can feel the difference between a campaign and a choice.

Chase one whale on purpose, not ten ghosts by accident.

3. "Same question as last month. Has anything changed on your side?"

He never varied the ask. Good morning, Colonel. I want to take Elvis on tour. The consistency did the selling. By the morning Parker finally engaged, he knew exactly what saying yes would mean.

A follow-up that reinvents itself every touch reads as flailing. A repeated unchanged ask builds weight every time it lands.

A repeated ask compounds. A reinvented one starts over.

4. "It's the first Tuesday of the month. You know why I'm calling."

Weintraub didn't call when he felt hopeful. He called at 8:30 because it was morning. The ritual carried him through a year of nos that would have wilted anyone running on mood.

Most reps do it backwards. They follow up when they're optimistic and vanish when they're discouraged, which means the pipeline moves on feelings.

Follow-up is a calendar, not a feeling.

5. "What would have to be true for this to become a yes?"

The morning Parker turned, he didn't say yes. He named a price. Be in Vegas tomorrow with a million dollars. A wall had just become a door with terms written on it.

Most stalled deals are walls nobody ever asked to price. Ask for the condition, and a vague no turns into a number you can work with.

A no with conditions is a yes with a price.

6. "You said it would take X. I'm here with X."

Weintraub didn't have the million. As he told it, he worked the phones all night, found an Elvis-loving radio man in Seattle, and walked into the meeting with a cashier's check.

Meeting a named condition fast is the loudest credibility move in business. When a buyer names the barrier and you remove it, say their own words back to them.

Meet the named condition and the deal closes itself.

7. "This one's worth a hundred calls to me. Most deals aren't. I did the math first."

A year of daily calls only makes sense aimed at the biggest name alive, and that's exactly where Weintraub spent it. He priced the pursuit before he started dialing.

Unlimited persistence on unqualified accounts isn't dedication. It's slow-motion quitting on the effort.

Persistence is an investment. Price the target first.
