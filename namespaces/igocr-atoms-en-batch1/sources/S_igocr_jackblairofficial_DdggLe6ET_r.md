Jack Blair

1. "Walk me through how it works today."

Rackham's observers sat in on 35,000 calls over 12 years. The background questions, what he called situation questions, did nothing for success. Buyers found them tedious, and the reps who asked the most of them were the least experienced. Ask one. Get the picture in the buyer's words, and stop before you ask a second. Everything you need from here comes from the problem, not the setup.

Background is for you. The buyer already knows it.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blain

2. "Where does that break down?"

A problem question gets what Rackham called an implied need: the buyer admits something is wrong. In small sales that admission was enough to win. In big ones it wasn't. The buyer had a problem and still didn't buy. Get the problem in their words and then do the hard part: don't fix it yet. Solutions offered this early are where the research found objections get made.

A problem admitted is not a problem felt.
<!-- image 3 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

3. "I might be reading too much into this. When that happens, what does it do to the rest of your week?"

Implication questions. The hardest to ask, and the ones that separated the top performers in large sales. They take a small admission and make the buyer see its size. Follow the problem downstream: time, people, money, the meeting they had to sit through because of it. Let them do the counting. Your job is the next question, not the total.

The cost the buyer says out loud is the only cost that exists.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. "Who else feels it when that goes wrong?"

Big deals, Rackham found, are decided by more than one person over more than one call. An implication that stays at the buyer's desk stays small. Ask who else pays for the problem, then ask them what they'd say about it. Now the problem has witnesses, and a problem with witnesses gets discussed at the meeting you're not in.

A problem with witnesses gets a budget.
<!-- image 5 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

5. "You're right, this isn't cheap to fix. What has living with it cost so far?"

The top reps in the research built the size of the problem before they even named a price. A price is only ever compared to something. A price is only ever compared to nothing. Get the buyer's number before you say yours. If they can't put one on it, help them build it: hours, months, deals lost. Then your price has something to stand next to.

Your price only looks big next to nothing.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

6. "If that went away, what would it be worth to you?"

Need-payoff questions. Rackham found the buyers who described the benefit themselves were the ones who bought. Their own words did what the rep's brochure couldn't. Ask it and go quiet. Write down the exact phrases they use. Those phrases are your proposal, your follow-up email and your close, in the order they said them.

The buyer's benefit list beats your brochure every time.

Jack Blair

7. "I'll let you say it, not me. What would you want this to do for you?"

That answer is an explicit need, and in the 35,000 calls explicit needs predicted the win. Closing techniques didn't. Rackham found the old closes hurt in large sales. When they answer, the stated by the buyer. Confirm it back, set the next step with a date, and don't add a close on top. The close already happened.

In a big deal the buyer closes himself, or nobody does.

Now look at where all seven started. 35,000 calls, and the reps who won never sold the fix until the buyer felt the cost.

Sound familiar?
