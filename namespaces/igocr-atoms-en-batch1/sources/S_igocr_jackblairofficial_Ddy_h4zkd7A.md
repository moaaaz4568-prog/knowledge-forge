GEORGIA TECH TESTED 20,000 PHRASES ON 45,000 KICKSTARTER PITCHES TO SEE WHY STRANGERS GIVE MONEY. THE WORDS EXPLAINED MORE THAN HALF OF WHO GOT FUNDED. HERE ARE THE 7 PHRASES THAT GOT PEOPLE TO GIVE:

1. "We built this for people who do the job themselves, not for the ones with a sales floor."

In the study, pitches that said "to build this" and "accessible to the" got funded. They named the thing was for and who it was not for, and the backers who fit stepped forward. The researchers filed it under social identity: people give to a version of themselves. On a call, that is the first sentence after hello. Name the buyer's tribe before you name a in the buyer's words before you name a feature, and say who it is not for out loud.

Nobody buys a product. They buy a version of themselves.

2. "By the fifteenth this will be running. Here's what happens on the first."

"Project will be" was the strongest phrase in the whole study, and "not been able" sat near the top of the failed pile with "the production costs" beside it. The pitches that got money spoke in a schedule. The ones that lost spoke in effort and expense. The researchers called it authority: a plan stated as fact sentence. So say the date, the first step and what the buyer will see by then, in the future tell what you have not managed yet.

Hope is not a schedule.

3. "Two owners your size started in September. Do you want their names?"

"Has pledged" predicted funding.
Someone visible had already said yes, and the next stranger followed. That is social proof, and the study caught it working on people with nothing but a web page to go on. On a call it has to be true and it has to be specific. Two named companies beat "hundreds of clients", and offering the names is the part that makes it believable, whether or not they take you up on it.

A crowd nobody can name is not a crowd.

4. "Whatever you decide today, you'll also leave with the plan on paper."

The authors' own example of a funded phrase was "also receive two". Backers were promised a second thing on top of the first, and they gave. The researchers filed it under reciprocity, and the gift was named before the money, not the audit. On a call, the gift is the thing the buyer keeps even if the answer is no: the written plan, the number you worked out for them. Say it, and say it as a certainty, not a favour.

Give the second thing before they've bought the first.

5. "Given the chance, would you start now or wait for the next opening?"
"Given the chance" and "option is" both predicted funding. The pitches that framed the offer as one option among a few, open now, got backed. The researchers called it scarcity, and they found it in pitches that never used the word urgent. So make the choice real and make it a question. Most offers have an actual start date, a cohort or a calendar, and asking about it lets the buyer feel the window without you inventing one.
Scarcity you have to invent is scarcity you don't have.

6. "Everyone who starts this month gets the recording, and a thank you with their name on it."

"Good karma and "was a top predictor of funding, and the authors singled it out. It was a reward line: give, and be thanked by name on the website. Reciprocity again, paid in recognition instead of product, and it worked on strangers. On a call, the smallest yes deserves a visible thank you too. The buyer who books the next step, sends the intro or answers the two hard questions gets named for it, and people come back to where they were thanked by name.

People return to where they were thanked by name.

7. "I'm trusting you'll see the value in this."

That one predicted failure. So did "not been able", "hope to get" and "even a dollar", which the authors said reads as grovelling for money. The pitches that asked to be trusted, hedged, or begged, lost. The funded ones never asked for trust. They gave the reason trust was unnecessary: a date, a number, a name. Cut every "trust me", "honestly" and "hopefully" from your calls, and put the fact you were protecting in its place.

Asking for trust is admitting you haven't earned it.

Now look at where all seven came from. A web page, a stranger, and a pitch with the right words in it.
