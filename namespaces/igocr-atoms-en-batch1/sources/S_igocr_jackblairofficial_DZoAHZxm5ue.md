1. “I did my homework on you before I walked in.”

Pete never had the easy charm Don and Roger threw around, so he showed up knowing the client's last three campaigns, their competitor's numbers, and the name of the guy who actually signed off.

Preparation reads as respect. A buyer can tell inside a minute whether you studied them or you're running the same pitch you ran at lunch.

The reps who wing it lose to the one who did the reading. Every time.

2. "Think about the version of this where you look brilliant for saying yes."

Pete understood something the slick guys missed. The person across the table isn't buying for the company, they're buying for the boss they have to face on Monday.

Nobody signs off to hit a metric. They sign off so they can walk into a room and look like the one who called it.

Sell them the story they get to tell upstairs and the deal closes itself.

3. "Let's stop selling you something clever and give you what you actually asked for."
Pete sat through years of pitches where the room fell in love with its own idea and the client quietly checked out.
The client already told you what they want. Your job is to hand it back to them, not to win an award for reinventing it.
Ego in the pitch is the most expensive habit in sales.

4. "I'll call you Monday. And I'll actually call you Monday."

Half the deals Pete closed, he closed by doing the boring thing nobody else bothered with: following up exactly when he said he would.

The pitch gets the attention. The follow-through gets the signature. Most reps go quiet the second the meeting ends.

Reliability is so rare now it closes like a superpower.

5. “Everyone here wants the big logo. I'll take the client who picks up the phone.”

While the stars fought over the trophy accounts, Pete quietly built a book out of the clients nobody wanted to babysit.

The overlooked prospect has no other rep chasing them, signs faster, and stays longer. The glamorous one has ten people in the room.

Go where the competition isn't.

6. “I’m not the name on the brochure. I’m the one who gets it done.”

Pete made peace with not being the showpiece and turned it into his actual pitch.

Buyers remember who picked up at 9pm when the account was on fire, not whose face was in the company deck. The workhorse earns the renewal the showman never sees.

Over a full year, quiet competence outsells charisma.

7. “So are we doing this, or do I come back next week and ask again?”

Pete was never too cool to ask for the business out loud, and never too proud to come back when the answer was “not yet.”

Most reps build the entire case and then never actually ask. The close is just the question, said plainly, without flinching.

Ask. Then be willing to ask again. That alone beats half your competition.
