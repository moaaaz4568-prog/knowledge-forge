1. "Let me argue against my own product for a minute."

For four centuries the promotor fidei attacked every candidacy on purpose, because a claim that survives attack is the only claim worth canonizing. Steelman the case against your own offer, out loud, before the buyer does it silently. What survives is suddenly credible.

An offer that survives attack sells itself.

2. "What would your most skeptical colleague say about this?"

Every sainthood case faced a professional skeptic, so no case arrived at the verdict unexamined. Your deal has a skeptic too. Summon them on purpose, answer them while you are present, and stop losing deals in meetings you never attend.

The objection you invite cannot ambush you.

3. "Here's the honest case for doing nothing."
The advocate's job was to make the strongest case for the other side, not a weak one. Do the same for the status quo. Lay out what staying put genuinely saves them, then price what it costs. A buyer who hears you argue both sides stops guarding the no.
Steelman the no and the yes gets heavier.

4. "If this fails for you, here's exactly how it would happen."

The advocate hunted for the failure mode in every miracle. Run the same autopsy before the purchase instead of after. Name the two ways this goes wrong and the guardrail do not fear risk. They fear risk nobody has mapped. A mapped risk is a reason to proceed.

5. "Push back on that. I want to see if it holds."

Four hundred years of canon law said a claim untested is a claim worth nothing. Inviting the attack flips the whole frame: you stop being the seller defending a pitch and become the examiner of your own evidence. Confidence that asks to be tested reads as proof.

Invite the attack and you own the courtroom.

6. "That's a fair hit. Here's what it actually costs you."
The advocate's objections were answered, never denied, and the answered case became unshakeable. Conceded case weakness fully, then weigh it against the cost of the alternative. A conceded flaw buys credibility that no amount of polish can, and credibility is what closes cases.
Concede the flaw, win the verdict.

7. "Don't buy the version you heard from me. Buy the one that survives your questions."

The Vatican's lesson in one line: a yes that was never argued against is worthless, and everyone quietly knows it. Hand the buyer the examiner's chair and let the offer earn the verdict. The deals that survive questioning are the ones that never come back unwound.

An unexamined yes cancels itself later.
