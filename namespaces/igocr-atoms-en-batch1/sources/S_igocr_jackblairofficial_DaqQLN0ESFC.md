1. "Let me think it over.' Sure. Which part needs the thinking?"
In 1974, linguist Keiko Ueda catalogued sixteen ways people in Japan decline without the word no. Delaying the answer is one of them. In a Tokyo boardroom, "we'll consider it" is understood as a burial.
Your buyer means it that way more often than your CRM admits. So probe for content. A real maybe has a subject.
A maybe with no content is a no.

2. "The replies stopped two weeks ago. I'll treat that as an answer unless you tell me otherwise."

Silence made Ueda's list, because silence is a complete answer with zero words. Most reps translate it as busy. It usually isn't.

Name it once, calmly, with a door in both directions. The busy come back fast. The ones who were gone were gone anyway.

Silence is an answer. Read it back once.

3. "Good question. And after I answer it, mine is still on the table."

Answering a question with a question is a catalogued dodge. The topic moves, and the no never has to exist.

So take their answers seriously, answer it clean, then walk straight back to you. One deflection is normal. A second one just told you where the deal actually stands.

Deflection is data. Bring your question back.

5. "Might be difficult" usually means it's dead. If it's dead, let's say so and part friends."

Translate the softer out loud and hand them a graceful exit. Watch how fast the truth arrives.

"Difficult" is a no in a nicer suit.

7. "Tell me no. I can work with a no. A maybe costs us both a month."

Ueda's catalog exists because saying no feels expensive to humans everywhere, not just in Japan. Buyers dodge that cost by handing you a counterfeit maybe, and you pay the bill in follow-ups.

Price the maybe out loud. Most people would rather be honest than expensive.

A maybe costs more than a no ever will.
