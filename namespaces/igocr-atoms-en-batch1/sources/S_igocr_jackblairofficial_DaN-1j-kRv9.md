1. End on a question they can only nod to.

Edwards called it the tie-down. You attach a tiny question to the end of a statement so they agree out loud. "Saving three hours a week would change your month, wouldn't it?" Every small yes makes the big one feel like it already happened.

Stack enough little yeses and the close is just the next one.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. Never ask yes or no. Ask which.

His most copied move, the alternate of choice. You don't ask "do you want it." You ask between two versions of yes. "Do you want to start Monday or the first?" Both answers are the sale. You moved the decision from whether to which.

Give two doors. Both lead inside.

3. Act like they already said yes.
The assumptive close. Stop asking permission and start handling the next step out loud. "I'll get the paperwork over to you this afternoon." Said calmly, it gives a ready buyer a clean path to just let it happen.
Confidence isn't loud. It's assumed.

4. Turn their question into the close.
The sharp-angle close. They ask "can you get it done by Friday?" You don't just say yes. You say "if I can, are you ready to go ahead today?" Their own request becomes the thing that closes them.
Answer a buying question with a closing question.

5. Shrink the number until it's nothing.
Edwards taught breaking price down to
its smallest honest unit. Not "two
thousand a year." "About five dollars a day,
less than your lunch." The total scares
people. The daily slice feels like nothing
they can't handle.
People don't fear the price. They fear the
size of it.

6. The first no is not the answer.

Edwards drilled that most sales close well after the prospect first says no. Not by pushing harder, but by staying calm and asking what's really behind it. The first no is usually a flinch, not a decision.

A soft "what's really stopping you?" outsells any hard pitch.

7. "Let me think about it" is a door, not a wall.

His handler for the stall. "Totally fair. Just so I'm clear, what part are you thinking over, the timing, the fit, or the money?" You turn a vague exit into one specific objection. And one specific objection is something you can actually solve.

Vague stalls kill deals. Name it and you can close it.
