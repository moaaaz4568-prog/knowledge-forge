1. "Most people in your spot ask me for the smaller version. I don't think that's you."

Jaime Escalante's entire result came from raising the ask on kids everyone else was lowering it for. Only three percent of American students even attempted AP Calculus. He aimed a barrio classroom straight at it, and they rose to the size of the expectation.

Buyers shrink to the offer you show them. Showing the smaller package deals get small.

Shrink the ask and you shrink the buyer.

2. "If easy worked, you wouldn't be on this call."

Garfield High had already tried easy. Low expectations, watered-down classes, a school that nearly lost its accreditation. Escalante's offer was the opposite of comfortable, dawn sessions and Saturdays and summer school, and the hard version is the one that finally moved the numbers.

When a buyer flinches at the effort, don't soften it. Point at the easy attempt already behind them.

3. "I could make this easier. I'd also be making it worth less."

Colleagues told Escalante the kids couldn't handle the prerequisites, so teach down to them. He refused, because the value was the difficulty. Passing AP Calculus meant something precisely because nobody handed it to you.

Buyers will ask you to strip out the demanding parts of your offer. Every piece you strip discounts the exact outcome they came for.

What costs nothing to start is worth nothing to keep.

5. "I want this commitment in writing from both of us. Not legal. Personal."

Escalante made the deal explicit before the teaching started. Students and their parents signed on to an hour before school, staying until five, and Saturdays. The commitment wasn't paperwork. It was the moment spectators became participants.

On big-commitment offers, put the mutual promises on one page and both of you sign it. Watch what happens to onboarding.

Signatures are cheap. Mutual ones aren't.

6. "This is hard for about three weeks. Then hard becomes your normal."

Escalante's room ran on ganas. The sign over his blackboard said desire because he knew the work stops feeling like a burden once it becomes identity. His students didn't endure calculus. They adopted it.

Forecast the adaptation, not just the effort. Buyers quit when hard feels permanent. They push through when hard has an expiry date.

Hard is a phase. Quitting makes it permanent.

7. "People sitting exactly where you are finished this. The pattern is on my side."

The record built itself into proof. Two passers in 1978. Seven in 1980. Fourteen in 1981. Eighteen in 1982. By 1991, six hundred Garfield students were sitting AP exams. Nobody had to believe Escalante anymore, they just had to look.

When belief is the objection, stop arguing the offer and show the pattern of people who started in their seat.
