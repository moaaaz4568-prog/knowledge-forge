1. "Let me put that in plain English."

Luntz's most repeated finding: long words don't impress people, they raise suspicion. The buyer hears jargon and thinks "what is this guy trying to sell me?" Plain language reads as honest. Complicated language reads as cover.

Simple sounds true. Fancy sounds like a trick.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "Tell me what you took from that."

His core law is that it's not what you say, it's what people hear. Every buyer runs your pitch through their own fears and assumptions, so the message that lands is never the one you sent. The only way to know what they heard is to ask.

Check what they heard. It's never what you said.
<!-- image 3 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

3. "Let me start with the part you care about."

Luntz proved order changes outcome. The same three points in a different sequence get a completely different reaction. Open on what matters most to them and everything after it gets heard. Bury it and nothing does.

Same points, wrong order, dead deal. Lead with theirs.

4. "Here's why this matters to you."
His rule: give people the why before the
therefore. Features without a personal
why just wash over the buyer. Once they
know why it touches their life, they lean
into the how instead of enduring it.
No why, no listening. The why buys you
the how.

5. "You're probably wondering what the catch is."

Luntz found that any doubt left unanswered gets assumed true. Every buyer has a silent objection running, and your silence confirms it. Say it loud first and it stops being their private reason to walk.

Name the silent doubt before it hardens into a no.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

6. Answer the objection. Never repeat it.

One of his cardinal rules: never repeat the criticism in your rebuttal. Echo their words, "no, this isn't overpriced," and "overpriced" is the only word they remember. Respond to the concern without ever re-saying it.

Repeat the objection and you advertise it. Just answer.
<!-- image 7 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

7. "Picture your first month after this is handled."

The slogans people remember for life are the ones they can see. Luntz found vivid pictures outlive every fact and figure, because people forget what you said and keep how you made them feel. End on an image, not a spec sheet.

Facts fade. The picture they can see is what closes.

Read those back. None of them is about talking better. They're about controlling what the other person actually hears, which one man spent three decades and 1,500 audiences proving is the entire difference between words that work and words that die.

And it's a purely human skill. Software can write the message. It cannot sit on a live call, hear what the buyer actually heard, and fix it in real time.

If you've been doing that for years for a number someone else capped, you already own the rare part. You've just never aimed it at a room that pays what it's worth.
