1. "Stop pitching and start a dialogue."

Richardson built her whole method on replacing the monologue pitch with a two-way conversation.

The old hard sell talked at people. She taught reps to talk with them, asking and listening until the buyer revealed the way in. A dialogue uncovers what a pitch only talks over.

Trade the script for a real conversation and they open up.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "What's driving this for you?"

Richardson trained Wall Street reps to dig for the need behind the need.

The first answer a buyer gives is rarely the real one. This question moves past the surface ask to the actual motive, and the motive is what you close on. Sell to the reason underneath.

Find what's really pushing them and aim everything there.

3. "Let me make sure I understand."
Richardson taught reps to play back what
they heard before moving on.
Reflecting the buyer's words proves you
listened and lets them correct you before
you build on a wrong assumption. It also
makes them feel understood, which is
when trust forms. Confirm before you
advance.
Repeat it back, and they trust you with the
rest.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. “Tell them what it means, not what it is.”

Richardson drilled reps to connect every feature to what it actually does for this specific buyer.

A list of features is forgettable. What sticks is what it means for their problem, their day, their number. Translate the fact into their language and it finally lands.

Don’t describe the feature, tell them what it changes for them.

5. "Earn the right to ask."
Richardson taught that hard questions
only get honest answers once you've built
trust.
You can't interrogate a stranger. Open
with warmth, show you're useful, and the
buyer gives you permission to ask the
deeper questions. Credibility comes
before the probing, never after.
Build the trust first, then the real
questions get answered.

6. "Check in before you move on."
Richardson taught reps to test for
agreement at each step instead of racing
to the end.
A quick "how does that sound so far?"
catches doubt while it's still small and
easy to handle. Skip it and you arrive at the
close carrying objections you never saw.
Confirm as you go.
Check they're with you before you take
the next step.

7. "What questions do you have for
me?"

Richardson closed by opening the floor,
inviting the buyer to surface whatever was
still unsaid.

The questions they ask tell you exactly
what stands between them and yes.
Answer those and there's nothing left to
block the deal. Their last questions are
your closing checklist.

Let them ask, and they'll hand you the
path to yes.
