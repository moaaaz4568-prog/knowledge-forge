1. "Before I show you anything, let me tell you about someone who was sitting exactly where you are."

Neuroeconomist Paul Zak drew blood before and after people heard a story and found character-driven stories release oxytocin, the me chemical that makes a stranger trust you. A feature list releases nothing. Open on a real person, a past client with a name and a face, not a spec sheet. The brain leans in for a character. A feature list gets forgotten. A character gets followed.

2. "For a year and a half, nothing worked. It got worse before it got better."

Zak found a story only releases the chemical if it first holds attention, and attention comes from tension. The flat version, same person, no struggle, held nobody and moved nobody. Don't sprint to the happy ending. Sit in the problem long enough that the buyer actually feels it before you resolve it.

No tension, no attention. No attention, no sale.

3. "You're going to recognize yourself in about thirty seconds."

Zak calls it narrative transportation, the moment the listener stops hearing about the character and becomes them. That's when the trust chemical spikes. Tell it so your buyer sees their own situation in the story, their own stakes, their own fork in the road.

They don't buy when they hear the story. They buy when they enter it.

4. "He had one move left, and if it missed, that was the whole business."

Zak's lab found the tension that drives the response needs real jeopardy, something genuinely on the line. A story where nothing is at risk is a home video: pleasant, forgettable, chemically dead. Name what the character actually stood to lose, and the room goes quiet.

No jeopardy in the story, no stakes in the room.

5. "The numbers are on the next slide. But the numbers aren't why he stayed."

Zak found emotional transportation, not information, is what predicts whether people act. Facts light up the language part of the brain. A story lights up the parts that actually decide. Lead with the human turn and let the data arrive after, as permission, not as the pitch.

Facts give them permission. The story gives them the reason.

6. "Ninety days later, everything was different. I'm going to let that sit for a second."

The arc has to resolve. Zak found the payoff, the release after the tension, is what seals the empathy. Land on the after, the changed situation, then stop talking. Silence right after a resolved story is the most persuasive sound on a call.

Land the transformation, then let the silence work.

7. "So. Does that sound like where you want to be a few months from now?"

Zak's clearest finding: the oxytocin a story releases predicts action. People who felt it gave their own money away, unprompted. A transported buyer has half closed themselves already. You don't push after the story. You just point at the door they walked toward.

Tell it right and the story does the asking for you.
