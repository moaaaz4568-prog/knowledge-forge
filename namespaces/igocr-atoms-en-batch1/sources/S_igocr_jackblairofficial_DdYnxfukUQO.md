RALPH LAUREN STARTED OUT SELLING TIES FROM A DRAWER AT DOUBLE THE PRICE OF EVERY OTHER TIE. $400K IN YEAR ONE. $8 BILLION A YEAR NOW. HERE ARE 7 PHRASES FROM HIS PLAYBOOK THAT WORK ON ANY BUYER:
<!-- image 1 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

1. "It costs twice as much because it isn't the same thing."

His first ties sold for $7.50 to $15 when most ties went for $3 or $4. When the buyer says the other guy is cheaper, agree with them. Then name the two things they're not getting for that price. Only if it's true.

A price you can explain never needs defending.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "Let me show you the whole look, not just the tie."

He never sold a tie as a tie. Within two years it was a full menswear line from clothier Norm, backed by $50,000 from clothier Norman Hilton, and by 1969 Bloomingdale's had built him a shop inside the store. He was selling the whole man. The tie was the way in. Stop quoting the unit. Walk your buyer through what their week looks like once all of it is in place.

People don't buy the part. They buy the picture.
<!-- image 3 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

3. "I know it's wider than what you carry.
That's why it'll sell."

Going wide was the whole idea. Ties in
1967 were narrow, his were not, and he
walked them into stores himself, one
buyer at a time, until the width was the
reason people asked for him. Whatever
your buyer flags as different, don't sand it
down. Say why the difference is the point.

The objection is usually the feature.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. "Start with the one store. If it moves, we talk about the flagship."

His ties didn't start on Lexington Avenue. Bloomingdale's put them in the Meadows branch out in Queen the Fresh sold, and then the flagship followed. He took the small yes and let the result make the big one. When a buyer won't sign for the whole thing, shrink the first step until they can, and put the review date on the calendar before you leave.

A small yes that works beats a big maybe.
<!-- image 5 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

5. "You won't be the first. Here's who already carries it."

Deliveries from that drawer went to Paul Stuart, Neiman Marcus and Louis of Boston, the best men's stores in the country. After that no buyer was gambling on a kid from the Bronx with a box of ties. They were catching up to the stores they already copied. Before your next call, write down the two names your buyer respects most who already said yes. Lead with them.

Nobody wants to be first. Everybody wants to be next.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

6. "You're not buying a tie. You're buying who you want to be."

"I don't design clothes. I design dreams."
His words. He named the line Polo, not Lauren, because the name sold a world before the tie ever left the box. Ask what your buyer wants to be true about their life a year from now. Sell them that. The product is just the ticket in.

A premium is never for the thing. It's for who it makes you.

7. "Now that the ties are working, let me show you the suits."

In 1968 the tie accounts became suit accounts. By 1971 there was a women's line and a store on Rodeo Drive. The buyer who said yes once kept saying yes. The easiest sale you'll ever make is the second one to a buyer whose first one worked. Book it before you leave.

Now look at where all seven started. A drawer in a borrowed showroom, a box of ties, and a guy who could sell.

Sound familiar?
