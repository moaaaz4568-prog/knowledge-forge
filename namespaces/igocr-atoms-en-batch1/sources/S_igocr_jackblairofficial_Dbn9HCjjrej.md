1. "BEFORE I SHOW YOU ANYTHING, WHAT'S YOUR READ ON THIS ALREADY?"
Jerry Avorn's team started every visit the same way.
Ask what the doctor already believes and why,
before presenting a single fact. They called the
method academic detailing.
Open your next call by asking for their current
thinking, out loud, in full. Then aim at that. You are
not correcting the market. You are correcting one
person.
You can't fix a belief you never asked to hear.

2. "I GET PAID IF YOU SAY YES. SO CHECK ME
AGAINST SOMEONE WHO DOESN'T."
The Harvard visitors were clinical pharmacists with
nothing to sell, and they led with that. Their
credibility came from naming their source and their
stake up front.
You can't claim neutrality, so claim the opposite. Say
what you earn, then point them at a number you
didn't produce.
Disclosed bias sounds like honesty. Discovered bias
sounds like a con.

3. "THERE'S ONE DECISION ON THIS CALL.
EVERYTHING ELSE CAN WAIT."
Every visit in the trial carried one behavioral
objective. Not a category review. One change,
defined before anyone knocked.
Pick the single decision before you dial and say it in
the first minute. A call with three goals closes none
of them, and the buyer feels the sprawl.
Two objectives in one call is a conversation, not a
close.

4. "HERE'S THE CASE NOBODY IN THIS CATEGORY WILL MAKE OUT LOUD."
Avorn mailed what he called unadvertisements. Ads built to argue against the drugs everyone else was promoting, in the same format the reps used.
Make the honest case against the popular option your buyer is defaulting to. Not against your own product. Against the thing they'd pick if you vanished.
Say the sentence the whole category is paid to avoid.

5. "RUN IT AGAINST THE DEAL YOU LOST LAST
QUARTER. WHERE DOES IT BREAK?"
The visits were never lectures. Avorn’s people made
the doctor work a real case in the room, because
participation is what changed prescribing.
Hand the buyer a live example from their own year
and ask them to find the flaw. They’ll argue
themselves through the logic you were about to
recite.
A lecture gets remembered. A test gets believed.

6. "YOU'LL HEAR THE SAME TWO SENTENCES
FROM ME EVERY TIME. THAT'S DELIBERATE."
The detailers carried two or three essential
messages, repeated identically across visits. No
new angle, no fresh pitch, no rotating value
proposition.
Pick the two things you want remembering and never
reword them. Reps who change the message every
touch teach the buyer that none of it was true.
A story that keeps improving stops being a story.

7. "WHAT WOULD HAVE TO SHOW UP IN 60 DAYS FOR YOU TO CALL THIS THE RIGHT CALL?"
The program didn't end at agreement. They came back and reinforced the change against what actually happened after it.
Set the proof standard while they're still deciding, in their words. A yes survives the second thoughts that kill deals in week three.
Give the yes a way to prove itself and it stops wobbling.
