1. "I could walk you through seven versions of this. I'm bringing you two."

In the famous jam study, Sheena Iyengar's big table pulled 60% of shoppers. The small one pulled 40%. But only 3% bought from the big table. The small one converted 30%.

Big menus attract. Small menus sell. Cut the deck before the meeting, because curiosity is not intent.

The big menu draws the crowd. The small menu sells.

2. "Which of these can we kill right now?"

Iyengar found the why. More options raise the odds of picking wrong, and walking away feels safer than choosing badly.

So hand the buyer the knife. Every option they eliminate is a decision they just made, out loud, in front of you. The field shrinks and the fear shrinks with it.

Every option they kill moves them closer to one.

3. "One question first. It deletes half the list."

Her research on choosing found people can't process long lists, but they handle categories fine. Structure tames the pile.

So open with a fork. Done for you or in-house. This quarter or next. Fix it or replace it. One clean fork clears half the menu before you present a thing.

One good fork beats ten good options.

4. "You're not stuck on the price. You're stuck on the menu."

Iyengar studied retirement plans and found the same collapse. The longer the fund list, the more employees never enrolled at all. They walked past free money because the list was long.

So when a buyer stalls, don't sweeten the deal. Shrink the field. A discount just adds one more variable.

They're not saying no. They're saying too many.

5. "If you could only keep one requirement, which one survives?"
Overload is weighing everything at once.
Ten criteria, ten options, one tired brain.
Her work shows choice comes back the moment the load drops.
Force the ranking. Once they name the one thing that has to be true, comparison stops being a maze and becomes a sort.
Buyers who know their one thing can choose.

6. "Want me to take three of these off the table?"

Here's the strangest jam detail. Shoppers tasted the same amount at both tables, about 1.5 flavors. Interest was identical. Deciding is what broke.

Your buyer doesn't need more information. They need fewer doors. Ask permission to subtract, and watch relief cross their face when you do.

Selling by subtraction reads as honesty.

7. "Here's the short list. Everything on it works. Now it's just fit."

One more finding from the study. People who chose from the small set were happier with what they picked afterward.

So pre-vet the finalists. When every remaining option is one you'd stand behind, choosing wrong is off the table. The fear dies, the yes lands, and they like the deal more later.

Make wrong impossible and the yes gets easy.
