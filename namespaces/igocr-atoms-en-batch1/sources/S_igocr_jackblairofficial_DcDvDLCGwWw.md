"YOU WANTED THIS TO COST LESS. I GET IT."
The book's first rule: feelings do not vanish when you argue with them. They vanish when they are named. Faber and Mazlish built every script on it. When the buyer flinches at the number, do not defend the number. Name the want underneath the flinch first. An acknowledged feeling stops needing to be repeated.
Argue a feeling and it digs in. Name it and it lets go.

"I WISH I COULD DO THAT FOR YOU."
Their move for the impossible request: grant it in fantasy. I wish I could make that banana ripe right now. The child hears that the wish is shared even when the answer is no.
The buyer asks for last year's price. I wish I could hand it to you, and here is what I can do, keeps you on the same side of the table while the terms stay firm.
People accept a no that clearly wanted to be a yes.

"HERE'S WHAT I'M SEEING."
The book replaces commands with description. Not
clean your room. The milk is spilled. Description
hands the conclusion to the listener instead of
forcing it.
Swap you keep pushing this decision for here is
where we are: two calls, one proposal, a start date
three weeks out. Describe the deal and let the buyer
conclude what it needs.
A description invites a decision. An accusation
invites a defence.

05 / 10
"RED CUP OR BLUE CUP. YOU PICK."
The famous move: never whether, always which. A child fighting bedtime stops fighting the moment the choice of pajamas is as is theirs.
Never ask a wavering buyer whether to move forward. Ask which works, the 1st or the 15th. Both answers are a yes, and nobody feels steered, because the choice is real.
People fight orders. Nobody fights their own choice.

"ONE WORD: SHIPPING."
When lectures fail, the book says use one word.
Shoes. Homework. Being handed nothing to argue
with leaves nothing to argue.
Your buyer already knows the open item. Instead of
the four paragraph nudge, send the shortest
message that names it. The contract. Your CFO's
question. Thursday.
The fewer words you use, the fewer they can fight.

"LET ME PUT THAT IN ONE LINE YOU CAN FORWARD."
When saying it fails, the book says write it. A note on the door outlasts a shout up the stairs, because written words keep working after the voice stops.
Say it on the call, then send the one line. The buyer's boss never hears your voice. Your written line is what walks into the meeting you are not invited to.
Your voice stays on the call. A written line travels.

08 / 10
"WHAT SHOULD WE DO ABOUT THIS?"
The book's endgame is joint problem solving: state both sides' needs in a sentence each, then ask for ideas. The child's ideas get kept, so the child keeps the plan.
Deal stuck on terms: say what you each need, ask what should we do about this, and hand them the pen. Plans the buyer co-writes do not renegotiated.
A plan they help write is a plan they defend.
