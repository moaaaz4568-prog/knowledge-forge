1. "Before we argue about terms, let's agree on how we'll decide."

Vienna, 1814. The four empires that beat Napoleon had redrawn Europe, and defeated France wasn't invited. Its envoy, Talleyrand, arrived carrying one weapon he'd prepared in advance: a principle. Legitimacy, the rule that sovereignty can't be taken by force. It sounded neutral. It happened to protect France. And because the whole Congress claimed to stand on it, they couldn't refuse it. Propose the standard first.

Whoever writes the rules wins the argument.

2. "Who else got left out of this decision? I should be talking to them."

To get in the door, Talleyrand rallied the other excluded parties, Spain and the smaller treaty signatories, and argued the Congress had no legitimacy without all of them. The embarrassed victors backed down and even burned the minutes of their earlier meetings. Then he took his seat at the big table.

When you're locked out of a deal, find who else is locked out, and arrive representing more than yourself.

Locked out alone is weak. Locked out together is leverage.

The winners deadlocked, Prussia and Russia against Britain and Austria, over Poland and Saxony. Talleyrand had waited months for the crack, and then proposed the solution himself: bring France inside, and France breaks the tie.

A unanimous buying committee doesn't need you. A deadlocked one does. Stop pitching the room as one thing and find where it disagrees with itself.

Weeks after being let in, the nation with no leverage signed a secret defensive treaty with two of its conquerors, against the other two. The news alone forced the compromise. Nobody fired a shot, and France was a great power again.

In a divided room, the small vendor who commits to a faction beats the big vendor selling to everyone.

6. "I'd rather get this right than get it this week."

Talleyrand's most quoted advice to young diplomats, as it has come down through two centuries: above all, not to do too much zeal. At Vienna he let the autumn burn while the victors exhausted themselves on each other, and the deadlock came to him.

Eagerness is the one card a losing position can't afford. The rep sprinting to close by Friday hands over the only leverage the buyer needed.

Zeal is expensive. Patience gets paid.

7. "Let's take the next one off the conference line. Dinner. No agenda."

Historians note the Congress never held a single plenary session. The real business of redrawing a continent got done at salons, banquets, and balls, and nobody worked those rooms like the French delegation.

Formal settings make people defend positions. Tables dissolve them. The deal that's been stuck in the meeting room for a month is often one dinner from unstuck.

Deals defend themselves in meetings and relax at dinner.
