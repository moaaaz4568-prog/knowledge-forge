1. "There are two reasons this fits you. I'm not going to give you ten."

Richard Petty and John Cacioppo mapped the two roads a mind takes to yes. Their finding: an average doesn't add your reasons up. They average them. Psychologists call it the dilution effect.

So cap yourself out loud. Two strong reasons, stated like you chose them, beat ten that beg.

A case is an average, not a pile.

2. "Do you want the full walkthrough, or just the part that matters for you?"
The research splits buyers into two processing modes: deep and skimming. The pitch that wins one mode kills the other. Most reps never check which room they're in.
Ask. The deep buyer tells you where to go hard. The skimming buyer just told you not to bury them.
Match the depth or lose the buyer.

3. "I could list five more features. None of them are why you'd buy."

Naming what you're skipping does two jobs. It proves you have more in the tank, and it tells the buyer you respect their attention enough not to spend it.

Use it right after your best point lands, when the old habit says keep stacking. That's the moment stacking starts costing you.

What you skip out loud still counts for you.

4. "If I only got one sentence with you today, here it is."
Compression is proof of conviction. A rep who can say the whole case in one line has clearly ranked their reasons. A rep who can't is hoping volume does the ranking.
Build the sentence before the call. Say it slow. Then stop and let it sit there.
One ranked sentence outsells an hour of coverage.

5. "That deserves one honest answer,
not five defensive ones. Here it is."

Watch what reps do when an objection
lands: they stack rebuttals until the good
one drowns out the rest. Under scrutiny, the dilution
effect runs the same way in defense as in
attack.

One answer, the best one, delivered like
it's obvious. Then quiet. The stack is what
guilt sounds like.

Five defenses sound like one confession.

6. "Which one of those actually decides it for you? The rest are noise, right?"

Buyers dilute too. Three soft objections in a row usually guard one real one. Answering all three keeps the real one safely hidden.

Make them rank. Whatever they name is the deal. Whatever they drop it anyway.

Answer the objection that decides. Ignore the escort.

Late-stage stalls almost never come from

Say this calmly at the stall and wait. What
comes out is the close.

The last question in the room is the deal.

Read those seven back. Not one of them adds anything. Every single one removes something, because under real attention a case is an average, and everything weak you leave in pulls the strong stuff down.

Knowing what to leave out, live, while the buyer's depth shifts in front of you, is the actual craft. A script can't prune the itself mid-call. A moment a third reason becomes one too many. You can. You've been able to for years.

Here's the quiet part. That editing instinct took you years to build, and the room you use it in primes it like talking. Same skill, wrong ledger.
