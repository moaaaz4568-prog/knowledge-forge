1. "Deals like this usually land between eighteen and twenty two."

Adam Galinsky's Columbia research found the first number spoken pulls the whole negotiation toward it, explaining most of the gap between similar deals. Naming the range first costs nothing and sets the field. From that moment the buyer is negotiating inside your numbers instead of you living inside theirs.

The first number owns the conversation.

2. "It comes to $5,150."
Columbia's Malia Mason showed precise offers beat round ones. A buyer hearing $5,000 counters hard. A buyer hearing $5,150 assumes the number was built from information they do not have, and pushes less. Round numbers read as guesses. Precise numbers read as receipts.
Round numbers invite haggling. Precise numbers end it.

3. "Let me tell you why the number is what it is."

Galinsky found an anchor survives only as long as its logic does. So attach the reasoning before anyone attacks it: what it replaces, what it prevents, what it earns them. An unexplained number is a target. An explained number is a position, and positions hold.

A number with a reason is twice as heavy.

4. "Set that number aside for a second. What does waiting cost you?"

When the buyer anchors low, Galinsky's research says the pull only works if you negotiate from their number. Refuse the ground. Return to your target and to what their alternatives actually cost them, out loud. An anchor you never stand on cannot move you anywhere.

An anchor only holds if you tie yourself to it.

5. "Where does that number come from?"

Galinsky and Mussweiler proved the counterpunch: a first offer loses its pull when its rationale is challenged directly. So make the lowball explain itself, line by line, while you sit still. Numbers that cannot defend themselves start moving on their own.

Make the number defend itself.

6. "I can do $4,890. That's the move."

Mason's data showed negotiators using precise numbers concede less over time, because sloppy concessions signal a soft floor. When you move, move once, by an exact amount, with a reason attached. The slide stops the moment your movement looks engineered instead of emotional.

Concede in inches, and name every inch.

Across Galinsky's studies the first mover captures more of the range, and the polite rep who waits hands that edge away on every deal. Going first is not rude. It is the seat with the money conversation on your paper, not theirs.

Whoever goes first draws the map.
