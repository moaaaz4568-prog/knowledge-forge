Last year I sold my company to private equity, and the takeaways I had from that were three main things. The first thing is that people do not give a fuck about your revenue. It does not matter when you're selling your business. The thing that actually matters is your EBITDA, so how much you are profiting. Now, the thing is, is that a very tempting trap that people get into when they're starting their business, they're scaling their business, is they try to so heavily optimize for their revenue, right? They'll try to do 100, 000, 200, 000, half a million dollars a month, and they'll feel so fucking awesome about it. But the thing is, is if you're making $100, 000 a month and you're walking away with $10, 000 a month, you are doing worse than someone who's making $15, 000 a month and walking away with 12. So instead of trying to hyper optimize for how big of a number can I get each month, what you should instead try to optimize for is how much are you actually taking home, because that is genuinely the only thing that matters, regardless if you're just trying to make a living, travel, and have a cool, interesting life, or if you actually ever want to sell your company, you need to focus on the profit, not on the revenue, because revenue is purely vanity and profit is what actually matters. The second thing is that nobody's gonna fucking buy your business if the entire business will fall apart if you step out of it. Founder dependence is one of the main reasons on why people end up not being able to sell their business. Because if someone is gonna pay a million dollars to buy your company, and the second you leave, the company drops all of its profit, then it's not worth fucking anything. You need to build your business so that you can step out of it without having problems. Now, a good test for this is if you take a week-long vacation or a two-week-long vacation and don't work on your business, or at least work on it very minimal, does the business do the same amount? Does it do less? Or does it do more than when you were involved in it? Because that test is what's going to determine if you can actually sell it or not. And the final thing that people are looking at is the longevity of the business. If you started a business and it got to a million dollars a month within the first two months of starting it, but it's only been running for three, four months, the chance that someone's gonna buy that business for any decent multiple whatsoever is next to zero, because there's no longevity. There's no proof that next year it's gonna be making the same amount as this year. And people do not buy fad businesses. People do not buy businesses that spike extremely quickly and drop, because they are going to lose money on their investment.
