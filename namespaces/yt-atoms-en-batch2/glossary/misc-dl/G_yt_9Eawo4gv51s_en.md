# G_yt_9Eawo4gv51s_en — stay-at-home wife

## Term

A stay-at-home wife is the question Nick does the math on: at what income levels does it make sense to have a stay-at-home wife, and what income level does a stay-at-home wife need to be able to get at a job for her to make sense to work? The phrase appears 10 times in the source, beside school, year, sense, and kids. His assumptions are: the partner who works has a $200,000 a year income — in California that puts you at a little over a 30% tax bracket for a married couple, in Florida closer to the 25% bracket; the average cost of a family health care plan without subsidies, paid out of pocket because the husband is self-employed, is about $24,000 a year for a family of four, and if the stay-at-home mom takes a job that offers health insurance for the family, just subtract $24,000 across the board. The cost of child care has accelerated rapidly, and it's changed the math on the viability of having a two-income household with children: having the second income usually outweighed any costs of child care or school, but as the quality of public education has declined and the cost of daycare has risen, that changed. Even in the stay-at-home scenarios, the family hires a babysitter for at least 15 hours a week with younger kids, and still needs a second car — taking kids to the doctor and running errands in Ubers every day really adds up. His concrete break-evens are: with one school-age child, about $39K if you send the kid to a Catholic school and $55,000 per year if you send them to a private school that's non-Catholic — and if you have an ROI requirement of 20% on your human capital, that $65K now goes up to $78,000 a year. With three kids, it still covers most of it for Catholic school, over 70%, but for private school it might not make it to that level.

## When the Meaning Breaks

- Read as a lifestyle choice: it is math — a $200,000 income, the tax bracket, $24,000 of health care, child care, and school costs.
- Read as the second income always winning: the second income usually outweighed the costs, but declining public education and rising daycare costs changed the math on a two-income household.
