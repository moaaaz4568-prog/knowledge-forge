# G_yt_-9PXVqf1boY_en — stand-up

## Term

The speakers use "stand-up" for the live comedy craft itself — going on stage and speaking — which the conversation keeps calling the hardest thing and an incredibly difficult craft. The phrase appears 13 times, across a long talk about comedians' working lives: the first performance in Lafayette, five minutes with a flashlight meaning one minute left, and the move from acting into stand-up. Its neighbours are "comedy", "comedian", "joke", and "punchline": comedy is a very difficult working profession that many leave for the movies, a joke builds quiet tension and then breaks it with a punchline, and comedy hangs by a thread because timing is all very sensitive. The concrete rule is the open door: in stand-up there are no barriers to entry — you do not have to learn to play the ukulele to be a stand-up comedian, you can just go on stage and speak — and that is why people do not think of it as an incredibly difficult craft, which ruins the comedians' lives.

- Read as public speaking with jokes: the speaker tried the tools from a public speaking course in stand-up and it did not work, because they are completely different things — stand-up is its own craft, not a speaking format with jokes added.
- Read as an easy way in: the open door is the trap — no barriers to entry means anyone can go on stage and speak, and that is exactly why audiences underrate how difficult the craft is, while the comedians live with the cost.
