1. "Before you answer, I know this is a big ask."

Jia Jiang spent 100 filmed days hunting rejection and found the pattern that flipped nos: acknowledge the doubt before they raise it. Voicing the objection first signals you are not trying to trick anyone, and people relax toward yes. The ask lands softer when the doubt is already on the table.

Name the doubt before they do.

2. "Can I ask why?"
Jiang's core move after every no. Most
rejections arrive dressed as policy and turn
out to be preference, and the why exposes
which one you got. Half the time the
reason is fixable on the spot. The other
half, you leave with intelligence instead of
a bruise.
A no without a why is a guess.

3. "If that's too much, what could you do?"

The retreat ask. When Jiang's big requests died, his smaller follow-up often lived, because people want to give something once they have refused something. Never leave a no empty-handed. Shrink the ask on the spot and the door that just closed opens a crack.

There is always a smaller yes in the room.

4. "Is there someone who could?"
Jiang learned a no usually belongs to the
person, not the organization. The
gatekeeper who refuses can often name
the one who will not. One redirect
question turns a dead end into a referral,
and a referred ask starts warmer than a
cold one ever will.
One person's no is not the building's no.

5. "What would make this easier to say yes to?"

Jiang's conclusion after 100 days: the burden is on the asker to make yes comfortable. This question hands the buyer the pen. They name the terms, the timing, the missing piece, and suddenly you are negotiating the path instead of begging for the verdict.

Comfort is the currency of yes.

6. "Can I come back to you next quarter?"
Jiang treated every no as a snapshot, never a verdict. Circumstances rot faster than interest does. Booking the return visit inside the rejection converts a dead deal into a scheduled one, and scheduled nos have a way of softening while you are gone.
A no with a date on it is a lead.

7. "Worst case you laugh at me. Here it is anyway."

On day three Jiang asked a doughnut shop for Olympic rings made of doughnuts, an absurd ask, and they arrived free in fifteen minutes. Timid requests collect polite refusals. Timid ones get taken seriously. Audacious ones get taken seriously. If the ask is big, make it bigger and say it with a straight face.

Timid asks get polite nos.
