1. "Can I give you the current number on that?"

Wood and Porter showed 3,127 people eight real public statements that were wrong, with the speakers split evenly between the two parties. Every correction moved the average person toward the fact, including the ones that cut against their own side. Say the number plainly and once. Do not build a case around it first.

Nobody has ever been argued into a fact. They just get told it.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "Where did that figure come from?"

The belief that people dig in when corrected came from a 2010 study run on a small convenience sample of undergraduates, and the trade repeated it for a decade. Wood and Porter tested 52 issues on more than ten thousand people and could not reproduce it once. Before you argue with your buyer's number, find out who gave it to them.

Most wrong numbers are borrowed, not believed.
<!-- image 3 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

3. "You're right about the direction. The size is what's off."

In their second study, 2,801 people saw misstatements corrected against the same data, and the average person moved toward the fact regardless of which side the fact had spoken. Concede the half they have right before you touch the half they have wrong. It costs you nothing and it is usually true.

A correction lands at the size of the error, not the size of the person.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. "I'll say the same thing when it's my own side that's wrong."

Across that same study, in no case did a group reject a correction because it landed on someone they already agreed with. People took the hit on their own team as readily as on the other one. So correct your own product out loud, in the same flat voice, before you correct theirs.

The rep who only ever corrects the competitor is the one nobody believes.
<!-- image 5 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

5. "Do you want that in writing?"

In the third study, 977 people read corrections buried inside realistic news articles with a dateline and a headline, and for every single issue the readers came away agreeing less with the false claim. The correction did not work. Put it in a follow-up email and let them meet it alone.

Some corrections only land when nobody is watching them land.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

6. "Is it the way I said it, or is it the number itself?"

The fourth study asked whether the thing making people dig in. Across 18 question wording and correction, run on 1,333 people, no backfire showed up anywhere. So when your buyer pushes back, stop rephrasing it. Ask them straight which one it is.

You cannot fix with wording what is actually a disagreement.
<!-- image 7 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

7. "Now that the number's right, does the decision change?"

This is the part the authors are careful about. People took the correction and still did not always change what they wanted. You can accept that a number was wrong and decide the thing was worth it anyway. Winning the fact is not winning the deal, so ask.

Now look at what those seven actually are. Not one of them is a better argument. Every one is a live person deciding whether to trust the one in front of them.

Sound familiar?
