1. He gave before he asked.

Cialdini's first finding undercover: the pros gave something first, and the gift made the ask feel owed. I gave Claudio and asked for the one thing I can hand a buyer in the first five minutes that costs me nothing. What came back:

Three gives, ranked by obligation. The winner: a two minute read of their own setup before any pitch: "Eleven of your people share one admin password. I'd fix that today with the steps?" Then the turn: "Whether you hire us or not. Want me to get the call when that breaks?"

2. The small yes before the big one.

Once a buyer says something out loud, they act to stay consistent with it. The car lots got a small yes first and let the buyer's own words do the rest. I asked Claude for the ladder of small yeses before the price ever comes up. What came back:

Five rungs for a 20 person accounting firm: the cost of the problem ("fair to say nobody owns IT right now?"), the cost of leaving it, what they've tried, what fixed looks like, and when. The rung most reps skip is what fixed looks like. Skip it and the price lands on nothing.

3. Let other people do the convincing.

Social proof and authority: people copy people who like them and defer to people who know. I asked my proof to Claude to rebuild stories a buyer recognises as himself, plus one authority line that sounds like a fact. What came back:

Three stories under 60 seconds, each ending at the moment they decided: the firm that lost a week in tax season, the partner who paid a ransom, the office manager being the help desk. The authority line: "We run IT for 31 accounting firms in Ohio."
