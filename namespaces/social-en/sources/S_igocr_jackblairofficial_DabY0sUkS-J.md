1. "You know what, in a lot of cases you'd be right about that."

Franklin's first rule, in his own words: "I made it a rule to forbear all direct contradiction to the sentiments of others."

He noticed something brutal about people who win arguments. They get the victory and lose the goodwill, and the goodwill was the thing worth money.

When the buyer pushes back, agree with the part that's true before you touch the part that isn't. Contradiction closes ears. Agreement opens them.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "The way it looks to me, and I might be wrong, is this."

Franklin banned the words "certainly" and "undoubtedly" from his own mouth. He replaced them with "it appears to me" and "if I'm not mistaken."

Sounds weak. Worked like a weapon. He wrote that this one habit was why people accepted his ideas with less resistance for fifty straight years.

Certainty triggers the buyer's defense. Modesty walks straight past it. Soft frame, hard content.

@jackblairofficial

3. "Can I ask you a quick favor before we go on?"

Franklin had an enemy in the legislature. Instead of charming him, he asked to borrow a rare book from the man's library. The rival became an ally for life.

His maxim: "He that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another, than he whom you yourself have obliged."

People don't invest in you because they like you. They like you because they invested. Small asks build the eyes muscle before the big one.

4. "So if that's true, what does that change for you?"

Franklin trained himself in the Socratic method: never assert, ask. Let the other person walk themselves to the conclusion.

A conclusion the buyer states out loud is theirs. A conclusion you state is yours, and people fight it what's yours.

Stop telling the buyer what the problem costs them. Ask questions untill they say they themselves.
<!-- image 5 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

5. "Let's put both columns on paper. Everything for, everything against."

Franklin invented the pro and con list. His method for hard decisions: write both sides down, weigh them, cancel them out, see what's left.

A buyer's fear lives in the fog. On paper, most objections shrink to two lines against a full column of reasons.

When they say they need to think about it, don't leave. Build the list with them, live on the call.

6. "Forget the specs. Here's what changes in your week."

Franklin, verbatim: "Would you persuade, speak of Interest, not of Reason."

Two hundred fifty years later reps are still reciting features at people who only ever bought one thing: what tomorrow looks like with the problem gone.

Cut your pitch in half. Replace every fact about the product with a fact about their life after it.

7. "Don't take my word for anything. Watch what the first two weeks do."

Young Franklin hauled his own paper through the streets in a wheelbarrow so the whole town could SEE him working. He didn't claim industrious. He performed it in public.

Claims are cheap and buyers know it. Visible proof does the persuading while you stay quiet.

Stop asserting your offer works. Point to where they can watch it working.

Here's the uncomfortable part.

Franklin used these lines to move kings, rivals, and whole legislatures, and he called the skill by its real name: persuasion is speaking to interest. You use the same skill every day. On the phones, on the floor, across the desk.

The difference between you and him was never the ability. It's that he kept renegotiating what room his skill got used in, and most reps never do. The words are portable. The paycheck follows the room. 👉
