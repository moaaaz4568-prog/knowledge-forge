1. Agree first. Always.

Cardone's first rule on any objection: agree with the buyer before you answer them. You're right, it is a lot of money. The fight ends before it starts. I gave Claude the five objections I hear most and asked for the agreement line that opens each one without giving away the sale. What came back:

Five objections, five agreements. On "it's too expensive": "You're right. It's more than most people plan to spend on a yard." Claude's note: the agreement has to be true. Then the turn: "Can I show you what it costs to do it right?"

2. Price was never the problem.

Cardone says price is a myth. The buyer who says it's too expensive is saying they don't see enough value yet. I gave Claude my offer and the problem it fixes and asked for the cost of that problem in the buyer's own numbers, so the price has something to stand next to. What came back:

The cost of the problem next to a $9,800 irrigation install: the $1,400 reseed every spring, the $340 summer water bills, the Saturday mornings on a hose. Claude's line: "You're already spending about $2,100 a year not fixing this. The only question is whether you keep paying it."

3. Put it in writing. Ask twice.

He writes the proposal in front of the buyer and asks for the close twice, because the first no is a reflex and the second ask is where the deal lives. I asked Claude for the one-page proposal shape and the exact words of the second ask, so it sounds like confidence instead of pressure. What came back:

A one-page proposal written at the kitchen table, three line items, one total, the problem in the buyer's words, one date. The second ask, after the first no: "Fair enough. Set the money aside for a second. Is there anything about the plan itself you'd change?" Usually there isn't.

4. The buyer who says it's too expensive.

The last prompt makes Claude the buyer who says it's too expensive and means it. It asks for a discount three different ways, and grades whether I held the price or defended it. Here's what it does:
