1. "You keep ordering the small one. What's it for?"

In 1981 he was running U.S. operations for a Swedish coffee maker company when a small Seattle coffee company kept ordering his plastic cone filters. He flew out to see who was buying. It was Starbucks. Within a year he worked there. Your best lead is already buying from you in a way that doesn't add up. Ask about the odd order before you pitch anything. The order tells you what the buyer hasn't.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair

2. "Tell me the real reason it's a no. I'd rather hear it now."

He pitched 242 investors on an Italian style coffee bar in Seattle. 217 said no. A no you can't see inside is the one that ends the deal, so get the reason on the table before you answer at, then stop talking. The buyer who gives you the real reason has just told you what to sell.

A vague no can't be answered. A specific one can.

3. "What would have made that a yes?"

Two hundred and seventeen nos in a row would end most careers. He kept the 25 yeses, raised the $400,000 he needed, and opened the first Il Giornale in 1986. The math of a no is simple: it costs nothing unless you stop. When a buyer turns you down, ask what would have changed it, write the answer down, and take it into the next call.

The no is free. The lesson is in the why.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. "You said no to running it. Would you say yes to owning a piece of it?"

The Starbucks founders refused to run his espresso bars, so he asked them a different question. Starbucks put $150,000 into Il Giornale, the company he built to do it without them. The people who said no to the idea said yes to a share of it. When your buyer refuses the offer, change the shape of the yes: a referral, a later date.

A no to the offer is not a no to you.

5. "Before you decide, there's someone I want you to meet."

In 1987 the founders gave him 60 days to raise $3.8 million to buy Starbucks. Halfway in, an investor from his own company bid $4 million to take it from him. Schultz went to Bill Gates Sr., a Seattle lawyer, who walked into the rival's office and told him to stand down. The deal closed at $3.8 million. When a deal is slipping, don't argue harder. Bring the heaviest person you can.

The room is decided by who else walks in.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair

6. "Give me a date, not a maybe."

Sixty days. That was the window the founders gave him to raise the money, and the date became part of the deal. A deadline turned a maybe into a decision, and the decision came on August 15, 1987. Most reps let a buyer's maybe float for months. Put a date on it. Ask when the decision gets made, who makes it, and what happens that day if nothing changes.

A maybe with a date is a deal in motion.

7. "When you said no, was that no for now, or no forever?"

The founders who turned down his espresso bars in 1985 sold him the whole company in 1987. Same people, same idea, two years later. The no was never about him. It was about that day. Go back through every no from the last year and ask each one this question. Circumstances change faster than opinions.

Now look at where all seven started. A copier route in New York, a coffee bar nobody wanted, and a guy who could sell.

Sound familiar?
