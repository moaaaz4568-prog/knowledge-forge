1. "The next step is the pilot. Let's get it booked."

In Stanley Milgram's 1961 obedience studies, ordinary people kept going because a calm process carried them forward one step at a time. Buyers move the same way. Never end a call at a decision. End it at the next step, stated as fact, calendar open.

A process pulls harder than a pitch.

2. "We've run this rollout a hundred times. Here's what happens."

The gray coat worked because it signaled someone had done this before. Your version is pattern experience, spoken plainly and specifically. Not confidence theater. The map of week one, week four, and the first bump. Buyers follow experience the way subjects followed the coat.

Experience is the modern lab coat.

3. "That risk is on me. I'll own this step."

Milgram's darkest finding is the most useful one: people continued when the experimenter said he was responsible. Responsibility transfer is the strongest compliance lever ever measured. Used honestly, it unlocks stalled deals. Take real ownership of the scary step and watch the yes arrive.

Buyers say yes when the risk stops being theirs.

4. "Most teams wobble in week two. It's already in the plan."

Subjects hesitated at every scream and continued anyway, because the authority treated hesitation as part of the procedure. Do the honest version before it happens. Name the friction and fold it into the plan. A predicted problem reads as control. A named problem reads as chaos.

A named problem is half a close.

Recognition is authority you can prove.
