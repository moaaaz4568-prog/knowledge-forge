1. "This isn't a pitch. I mostly want to understand how things work on your side."

Hanns Scharff's first move was killing the adversarial frame. Prisoners braced for a fight got a friendly conversation instead, and guarded minds don't stay guarded without an opponent.

Open the call by dropping the seller role out loud. A buyer with nobody to resist starts talking.

Remove the opponent and the guard goes with it.

2. "I've seen a lot of setups like yours. Here's how it usually goes."

Scharff's signature was the illusion of knowing it all. He'd present what he already knew so completely that confirming details felt harmless.

Describe their world before they explain it. Every correction and every nod hands you intel a direct question would have spooked.

Show them you know the terrain. They'll fill in the map.

3. "My guess is price isn't the real holdup here."

Scharff almost never asked direct questions, because researchers found direct questions raise the source's alertness. He used claims instead.

Float your read as a statement and wait. Right or wrong, the buyer responds to it, and the real objection walks out on its own.

A claim gets an answer a question would scare off.

Treat the reveal as normal and they reveal more.

Scharff did his best work on casual walks, away from the interrogation room, because the setting itself kept prisoners defended.
