1. "Walk me through how you're handling this today."

Keenan starts every deal by mapping the buyer's current state in detail.

You can't sell a change until you know exactly what you're changing from. Getting the buyer to describe their present situation out loud is what makes the gap visible to both of you.

Map where they are before you sell where they could be.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair

2. "And where do you want this to be instead?"

Keenan pulls the buyer's desired future state into the open.

The space between where they are and where they want to be is the gap, and the gap is the only thing that actually sells. Once they describe both ends, the distance does the persuading.

Get them to name the future, then sell the distance.

3. "What's it costing you to stay where you are?"

Keenan makes the buyer quantify the pain of the status quo.

People don't buy until staying still hurts more than changing. Putting a real cost on the current state turns a nice-to-have into a problem they can't keep ignoring.

Price the problem and the solution sells itself.

4. "Why is that happening, do you think?"
Keenan digs past the symptom to the root cause before pitching anything.
Buyers describe symptoms; the sale lives in the cause. Diagnosing why the problem exists makes you the expert who understands it better than they do, and that's who they buy from.
Find the root, not just the symptom.

5. "Help me understand the impact on the rest of the team."
Keenan widens the problem from one person to the whole operation.
A problem that only annoys one person is easy to defer. Showing how it ripples across the team and the numbers raises the stakes and the urgency at the same time.
Expand the problem and you expand the reason to act.

6. "Based on what you told me, here's the gap I'm seeing."

Keenan reflects the diagnosed gap back before he ever talks features.

When you name the gap in their own words, the buyer feels understood, not sold. Now the product isn't a pitch, it's the bridge across a gap they just described themselves.

Sell the bridge across their gap, not the product.

7. "If we could close that gap, what would that be worth to you?"

Keenan lets the buyer attach the value instead of stating the price.

When the buyer names what solving it is worth, price stops being the fight. They've already decided the gap is expensive, so the solution becomes the obvious math.

Let them set the value and price takes care of itself.
