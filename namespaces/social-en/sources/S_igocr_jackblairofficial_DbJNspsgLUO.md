1. "What made you actually take this call?"

Arthur Aron sat strangers together in his lab with 36 questions that climbed from surface to serious. Forty five minutes later they rated closer than pairs given the same time of small talk. The climb starts with one honest rung. Not how are you. Why are you here.

Small talk is a staircase to nowhere.

2. "What's the version of this that actually excites you?"

Aron's questions worked because each answer disclosed a little more than the last. Excitement is early-rung disclosure: safe to say, revealing to hear. A buyer describing the version they want is handing you the deal's blueprint while feeling like they're just talking.

Let them describe the yes first.

3. "What almost stopped you from booking this?"

The experiment's engine was reciprocity: people match the depth they receive. This question invites the first real confession, and it lands best when you go first, one honest line about why deals like this stall. Depth offered is depth returned.

Go one rung deeper and they follow.

4. "Who's counting on this working besides you?"
Aron's ladder climbs toward what matters to people, and what matters is almost always other people. Ask who else feels this problem and the buyer stops defending a budget and starts describing a responsibility. Deals move when the answer has faces in it.
Budgets don't feel urgency. People do.

5. "What's the part you'd never put in an email?"

Aron ran his ladder face to face for a reason: people will say what they would never write. Calls exist for the unwritable part. Name that directly and you give the buyer permission to say the real constraint. The unwritable part is the deal. The real objection has never been typed.

6. "A year from now, what would make you glad you did this?"

The ladder's power is projection: each rung has the person describing themselves, not reporting facts. A buyer projecting a year ahead is rehearsing life after the yes, and people work to make their own predictions true. You are not asking for a forecast. You are installing one.

People defend futures they described themselves.

7. "What should I have asked that I didn't?"

The deepest questions in Aron's set work because they let the other person steer. The last question of a great first call does the same: it gives the buyer the floor for whatever the script missed. What comes back is the thing they were waiting to be asked.

The best last question is an open door.
