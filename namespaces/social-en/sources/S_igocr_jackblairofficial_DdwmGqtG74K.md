SHE STARTED SELLING OFFICE SPACE AT 36.
NEW YORK'S DEAL OF THE YEAR, 11 TIMES.
A RECORD SINCE 1944.
HERE ARE HER 7 PHRASES FOR ANY BUYER:

Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

1. "I read up on your business before I dialed. Can I tell you what I think is going on?"

Her name is Mary Ann Tighe. At 36 she was an art historian with no business background, and Ed Gordon, a legend of the New York market, turned her down flat. So she wrote a magazine profile of him instead. It ran, and he hired her a month later: anyone who understood his business that well could sell it. Before your next call, learn the buyer's business well enough to say one true thing about it they haven't heard. Open with that.

Buyers don't want to be pitched. They want to be understood first.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "Who actually gives the yes on this? If it isn't you, let's get them on the call."

Her rule, in her own words: "Who is it that can actually say yes? If you're not talking to that person, you're not going to get a yes." A 1.1 million square foot lease has a dozen people who can say no and one who can say yes. Most reps pitch call and find out on the follow-up that the decision lives somewhere else. Ask inside the first ten minutes. If the answer is a name you haven't met, your next job is that meeting, not a proposal.

A yes from the wrong person is a no you haven't heard yet.

Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

3. "Before we get into the deal, tell me about you."

Government taught her this one. On Carter's White House staff, a colleague she thought did nothing but wander the halls talking to people got her a meeting with the chief of staff that she couldn't get herself. "Personal relationships trump everything else," she says, and the first five minutes of her calls are about the person, not the space. Ask about the buyer before you ask about the budget, and moves at the speed of you.

People sign with the person who knew them before the ask.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. "Nobody's built here in ten years. That's the reason to go first, not the reason to wait."

Her first deal that was entirely her own was 4 Times Square, 1996: Condé Nast, the publisher of Vogue, into a new tower on a Times Square no people still avoiding New York had put up in a decade, and it was the first building New York had put up in a decade, and I did it." It started the redevelopment of Times Square. When your buyer says nobody in their industry has done this yet, agree with them. Then show them what being first is worth before everyone else arrives.

Empty isn't a warning. Empty is a head start.
<!-- image 5 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

5. "It's not the price. It's that you can't picture it yet. So come and look."

Downtown after 9/11, nobody would take an office, and no argument moved them. She asked a psychiatrist why. People fear the unknown, he said. So she stopped arguing and made the site familiar: fashion shows, art exhibits, anything that got people to come and stand there. Condé Nast later signed 1.1 million square feet at One World Trade Center. When a buyer stalls, the fix is rarely another point. It's a demo, a walkthrough, a call with a customer who already lives there.

A buyer can't say yes to something they can't see.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair

6. "I've decided I want to do this with you. Can we start?"

In 2002 she was vice chairman of her firm and believed her career had peaked. So she cold called the CEO of CBRE, the biggest real estate firm in the world, and asked him for a job. She has run its New York region ever since. That's the whole ask, and she made it without a warm-up. When you've earned the close, say it that plainly. Skip the slide, the soft landing and the "does that make sense." Tell the buyer you want them, and ask.

The direct ask only feels awkward to the people who never make it.

7. "You didn't ask for this one. Look at it anyway."

It was her idea to move The New York Times. The paper hadn't gone looking for a 52 story tower on Eighth Avenue; she brought them the option nobody was pitching, and it became a highlight of her career. The buyer's best deal is often the one they hadn't imagined until someone put it in front of them, and nobody else is pitching it. Bring one thing to every call the buyer didn't ask for.

Now look at where all seven started. A woman of 36 with no contacts, no deals for a year and a half, in a business that told her she didn't belong.

Sound familiar?

Read that again if you sell for a living.

Everything Tighe did before the meeting, a machine does now in seconds. The research on the buyer. The comps. The proposal. The follow-up.

Anything sold from a desk, off a script, on a screen is going to a machine.

The safest people in sales are the ones holding the one skill it can't fake: a live buyer says not yet, scared of what they can't picture, and you get them to come and look, and then to yes.
