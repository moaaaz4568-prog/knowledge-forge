1. "Before we talk about signing anything, I want you to actually use this."

Harry Gordon Selfridge invented modern shopping, and his first radical move was taking goods out from behind the counter and letting people touch them. Before him you asked a clerk. After him you held it yourself, and holding a thing is halfway to owning it. Get it into their hands, their trial, their calendar, before you ever ask for a decision.

What they hold, they've half bought. Put it in their hands first.

2. "Let me show you what this actually makes possible before we get anywhere near a number."

Selfridge’s line was: excite the mind and the hand will reach for the pocket. He never led with price. He led with the vision that made the price feel small. Paint what their life looks like on the far side of yes, in detail, before a single figure enters the room.

Excite the mind first. The hand reaches for the wallet on its own.

3. "You're not really buying this. You're buying what stops being a problem the day it's yours."

His whole empire ran on one idea: nobody wants the object, they want how the object makes them feel. He sold the afternoon, the pleasure, the status, never the glove itself. Name the feeling waiting on the other side, not the feature on the invoice.

Nobody wants the thing. They want who they get to be with it.

4. "Here's what changes if this is done by the first, and what it quietly costs if it slips."

Selfridge invented the shopping-days-until-Christmas countdown, the first real deadline in retail. Not fake pressure, a true clock. Every buyer moves faster against a date that actually means something. Find the real deadline already sitting in their world and say it out loud.

A real deadline closes what a great pitch only warms up.

5. "Give me two minutes to show you this properly. It's worth seeing right."

He was the first to light his windows at night and turn a display into a show, because he understood desire is manufactured by presentation, not just product. The same offer, presented like it matters, becomes something they want. Slow down and frame it like the thing it is, not a link buried in an email.

The offer isn't the product. It's how you make them see it.

6. "I'm not going to push you. I'm going to make this the easiest yes you've had all year."

Selfridge trained his floor to help, never to shove, because a cornered buyer buys nothing and a helped buyer comes back twice. Take the pressure out of the room. Be the person removing friction instead of adding it, and the buyer stops defending and starts deciding.

Push and they brace. Help and they buy.

7. "Tell me what a perfect version of this looks like for you, specifically."

He built an empire by obsessively studying what his customers actually wanted, then handing it to them before they asked. His own rule: sit up and take notice of what makes them sit up and take notice. The seller who knows exactly what this one buyer wants is already half closed. Ask until you do.

Notice what makes them lean in, then hand them exactly that.
