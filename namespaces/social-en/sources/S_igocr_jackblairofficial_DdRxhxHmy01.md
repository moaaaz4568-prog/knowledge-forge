1. Sell what they reach for first.

William Wrigley Jr. sold soap in Chicago.
To win over store owners, he threw in free
baking powder. They wanted the baking
powder more, so he sold that and threw in
free chewing gum.

They wanted the gum more. So he sold
the gum.

His family ran Wrigley for four generations.

What buyers get excited about tells you
what to sell.

2. Let them see the proof.

Henry Heinz started out selling vegetables and horseradish door to door as a boy. When he began bottling horseradish, other sellers used colored glass that hid cheap filler.

Heinz used clear glass. Buyers could see exactly what they were paying for.

His son and grandson ran the company for nearly 70 years after him.

Don’t tell buyers it’s good. Show them.

3. Ride along with the best closer you know.

Thomas Watson got off to a slow start selling cash registers for NCR in Buffalo.

So he rode along on sales calls with his manager, John Range, and copied what worked. Within two years he was the top salesman in Buffalo.

He went on to build IBM. His son ran it after him.

Stop guessing. Go watch the best one work.

4. Go straight for the biggest buyer.

Harvey Firestone started out selling buggies. When he moved into rubber tires, he went after Henry Ford after Henry Ford became his customer. The two families grew so close that Firestone's granddaughter married Ford's grandson.

Their son, Bill Ford, became chairman of Ford.

One yes from the right buyer outlasts a thousand small ones.

5. Sell the answer to the question they
keep asking.

Samuel Johnson sold parquet floors in
Racine, Wisconsin. Buyers kept asking him
the same thing: how do I take care of it?

So he started selling floor wax. Before
long, the wax was outselling the floors.

His great-grandson runs SC
Johnson today. Five generations.

The question your buyers keep asking is
your next product.

6. Solve the problem that shows up after
the sale.

Jack Taylor sold Cadillacs in St. Louis. His
customers had a problem: when their car
was in the shop, they had nothing to drive.

So he started a leasing company and
added rentals for exactly that. It became
Enterprise.

His granddaughter runs it today.

The sale isn't the end of the buyer's
problem. It's your next opening.

7. Watch what your buyers already do.

J. Willard Marriott paid his way through college selling wool underwear to lumberjacks. Years later, he noticed customers at his restaurant near a Washington airport buying food to take on their flights.

So in 1937 he started selling meals to the airlines.

His son ran Marriott for 40 years. His grandson is chairman today.

Your buyers are already telling you what they want. Watch them.
