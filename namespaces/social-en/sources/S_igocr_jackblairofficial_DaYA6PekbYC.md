1. I'm curious. Tell me more about that.

King's rule was simple. He never learned anything while he was talking.

Every answer a buyer gives is data you can close on. Every sentence you add on top is data you lose.

Ask, then go quiet. The person who talks least on a call often controls it.

2. Why is that important to you?

King opened people up with short, plain questions and real curiosity, not clever ones.

Why questions move a buyer from what they want to why they want it, and the why is where the money is.

Chase the reason under the request. People buy for their reasons, not yours.

3. How did you end up in this spot?

King liked to start at the beginning and let people tell their own story.

Let a buyer walk you through how they got here and they hand you the problem, the stakes, and the opening, all in their own words.

Get the backstory first. The path in usually shows you the path to yes.

5. I want to make sure I've got this right.
King made guests feel heard by reflecting
back what he actually heard.
Playing back a buyer's own words proves
you listened and lets them correct you
before you pitch the wrong thing.
Summarize before you sell. A buyer who
feels understood stops defending and
starts deciding.

6. That's a good question. Let me be straight with you.

King built trust by being direct and admitting what he did not know.

A rep who answers a hard question honestly, even when it stings, earns more belief than one with a slick reply for everything.

Trade the perfect answer for the honest one. Trust closes what polish cannot.

7. What haven't I asked that I should?

King knew the best material often came
from the question he almost missed.

This line hands the buyer the floor and
surfaces the real objection hiding under
the polite ones.

Before you close, ask what you missed.
The deal often turns on the thing they
were waiting to be asked.
