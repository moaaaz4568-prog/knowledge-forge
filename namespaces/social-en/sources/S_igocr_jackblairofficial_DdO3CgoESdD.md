1. "How's that working for you?"

Dr. Phil's signature question, and his third life law in five words: people do what works. If the buyer is still doing it the old way, some part of it is paying him. Ask it flat, no edge, right after he describes the current setup, then wait. A buyer who has to grade his own status quo out loud stops defending it.

Make them grade the old way before you pitch the new one.

3. "What would make me the wrong call here?"

McGraw prepped witnesses for a living before television, and the witness who admits the weak point first is the one the jury trusts on the strong one. Say it when they get defensive. Invite the doubt, then ask what would fix it. Pressure drops, and the real objection walks out on its own.

Concede the doubt first and the trust comes with it.

Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. "What's the payoff for staying where you are?"

Law three again: people do what works. Nobody stays in a broken setup for no reason. There's a payoff: comfort, control, not having to explain a change upstairs. Ask it with real curiosity, no trap in your voice. Once the payoff is on the table you can price it against the cost they gave you. Now they're choosing, not stalling.

Every stall has a payoff. Find it or lose to it.

5. "What would you have to admit for this to be a yes?"

The move his show was built on. Law ten: you have to name it before you can claim it. Use it when they agree with everything and still won't move. The answer is usually "that I've wasted a year" or "that my guy isn't good enough." Let them say it. Then say nothing.

The admission is the close. The signature is paperwork.

6. "Who decided it has to work this way?"

Law eight: we teach people how to treat us. In McGraw’s frame, every situation you tolerate is one you trained. Say it when the objection is really resignation: "that's just how it is in our industry." Ask who decided that. The moment they can't name anyone, the rule stops being a rule.

A rule nobody wrote is a rule you can rewrite.
