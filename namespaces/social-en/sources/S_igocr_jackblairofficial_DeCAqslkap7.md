1. "I'm not the smartest person on this call, and I don't need to be."

Bill Gates hired him on 11 June 1980 as employee number 30, on $50,000 and a slice of the company, and he could not write a line of code. He was in a building full of people who could. His job was to get things decided. On a technical call, stop competing on expertise with the person who has it. Make the decision easy instead.

Nobody buys from the cleverest person on the call. They buy from the one who makes the decision simple.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "Who else in your company wins if this works?"

Ballmer's answer to almost every strategy question was one word said three times: developers. Microsoft did not win by selling to buyers. It won by arming the people who would build on top, because everybody one of them then sold it to somebody else. In your account won't there is somebody whose week gets better if you win. Find them on the first call.

The person who signs is rarely the person who sells it inside.
<!-- image 3 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

3. "The price isn't the problem. Let's get the shape of it right."

Under Ballmer, Microsoft stopped selling boxes one at a time and started selling agreements: multi year, company wide, with a renewal date. The money was similar. The shape made it easy to approve. When a buyer stalls on your number, change the terms before you change the price.

Most price objections are timing objections wearing a different coat.
<!-- image 4 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. "I got that wrong. Here's the real answer."

In 2007 Ballmer said in public that the iPhone had no chance of getting any significant market share. He was wrong in front of everybody and later said so plainly. Your buyer already knows when you have overstated something. Correcting yourself out loud, in the same call, costs you nothing and buys you the rest of the hour.

A rep who has never been wrong in front of a buyer has never been trusted by one.

Jack Blair

5. "I'll still be the one you call after you sign."
Ballmer did not only run sales. He ran support, the part of the company any a buyer meets after the money has already gone. He knew the second sale is decided by whoever answers the phone in month three. Name that person on your call, out loud, before the buyer thinks to ask.
A deal is rarely lost before the signature. It is lost in month three.
<!-- image 6 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

6. "Let's decide today, even if the answer is no."

Ballmer ran rooms at a volume people still talk about, and almost nobody walked out of one undecided. The volume is not the part to copy. The refusal to leave a room without an answer is. Ask for the decision before the call ends, and make no a perfectly acceptable one.

A clean no on Tuesday beats a warm maybe for a month.

Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

7. "You're not buying the software. You're buying whoever picks up in three years."

When Ballmer became chief executive in January 2000, Microsoft's annual revenue was under $23 billion. When he left in 2014 it was close to $78 billion, and most of it came from customers who had signed years earlier and kept renewing. Sell the next three years, not the next invoice.

The renewal is won on the first call.
