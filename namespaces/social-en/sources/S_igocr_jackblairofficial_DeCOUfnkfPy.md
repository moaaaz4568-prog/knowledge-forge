1. What made you comfortable?

O'Leary doesn't ask what the competitor offered or what they charge. He asks what made you comfortable with them. Comfort is the real reason and the weakest one to defend. I gave Claude my offer and the three competitors I lose to and asked for the question that turns their quote into a comfort blanket. What came back:

Three questions, one survivor: "What did they say that made you comfortable?" On a strip-mall roof the owner answered "they can start Monday." Claude's note: he just told you the real criterion is speed, not price. The follow-up: "And what happens to that start date the first time it rains?"

2. The pattern he's seen 200 times.

His move on a rival is a pattern, not an argument: I've seen this play out 200 times, here's what happens next. I gave Claude my last twenty deals, won and lost, and asked for the three patterns I can say out loud the moment a buyer names a competitor. What came back:

Three patterns from twenty roofs, one sentence each. The one that lands: "Every bid that came in $9,000 under ours carried a one-year workmanship warranty. All three of those owners were calling a different roofer by year three." Claude's rule: say the number, say what happened, stop talking.

3. Price is the wrong variable.

They'll match my price. They won't match my outcome. O'Leary never defends a number, he changes the variable the buyer is judging. I gave Claude my price and the result a customer actually gets and asked for the math that makes the cheaper quote the expensive one. What came back:

The math on a $38,000 roof against a $29,000 quote: a 25-year system is $1,520 a year, a 12-year one is $2,416, before the second tear-off. The line: "The cheaper roof costs you $900 a year more. You just pay it later, with a crane on site." Price never came up again.
