1. "Let's stop trading emails. One room, one afternoon, everyone who matters."

Richard Holbrooke called it lock-up diplomacy: three warring presidents housed steps from each other at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base until the deal was done. He knew they'd never agree at a distance.

Neither will your stakeholders. Deals scattered across email threads die of exposure. Assemble the decision.

A deal that lives in an inbox dies in an inbox.

2. "Neutral ground. Not your building, not mine."

The site was chosen deliberately to pull every party out of their comfort zone. Nobody negotiated from a home-field throne in Dayton.

When a deal stalls in the buyer's conference room, move it: lunch, a walk, a different floor. Territory is leverage, and neutral ground resets it.

Change the room and you change the conversation.

3. "Just us. No audience. People posture for crowds."

The talks ran under a total press blackout, and the reason is documented: Holbrooke wanted no posturing through leaks.

Committees work the same way. Nobody concedes in front of spectators. When a deal needs a real conversation, shrink the room until the performing stops.

Small rooms get truth. Big rooms get theater.

4. "We're not touching the hard one yet. Bank the easy three first."

His other documented method was step-by-step: small concessions agreed early, each one a stepping stone laid before the brutal issues. Momentum is a currency.

Stack the easy agreements out loud so that by the time you reach the deal-breaker, walking away means demolishing a staircase they helped build.

Every small yes makes the big no more expensive.

5. "Before you walk, let's list everything you'd be walking away from."

At the brink, Holbrooke's team made two giant posters in block letters: everything the Bosnian delegation had already gained and would lose if the talks failed. On the wall, in writing.

When your buyer wavers late, itemize the stack: the pricing, the terms, the work already done. Make the loss visible.

Nobody reads the cost of walking until you write it down.

6. "You two don't need me for this part. Talk. I'll be outside."

At the critical moment, Holbrooke pulled two principals into a room, asked them to negotiate face to face, and left. The deal wasn't about him, and he knew it.

Sometimes your champion and the decision-maker need the room without a rep in it. Set the meeting, then have the nerve to exit.

Know the moment the deal closes better without you in the room.

7. "I'd rather close the file today than pretend for another month."

On the final morning, the talks were genuinely shutting down. Bags packed, outside the doors, engines running. Hours later the last concession landed and the war was over.

The move worked because it was real. Never bluff a deadline. Be honestly ready to end it, say so plainly, and watch what surfaces.

A deadline you'd actually eat is the only one buyers can taste.
