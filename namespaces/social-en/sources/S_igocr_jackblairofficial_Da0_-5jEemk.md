1. "Give me ninety seconds of real attention. If it's not worth it, I'll get off the phone."

Apollo Robbins is the pickpocket who did it, and neuroscientists study him for one reason. He never grabs attention. He negotiates for it, then owns the window. Your buyer is on a call with you and an inbox at the same time. Set the terms of the window before you spend a word inside it.

Attention is the first thing you close.

2. "What's the number running in your head right now?"

Robbins uses questions to occupy the inner voice, because whatever's playing in someone's head drowns out whatever's coming in their ears. Your buyer started doing price math the second you said hello. While it runs, they hear nothing. Pull the number out into the open and the room goes quiet enough to sell in.

The voice in their head is louder than yours.

3. "Forget everything I just said. If you keep one thing, keep this."
Robbins says attention is a single spotlight. He moves one, never two. Reps run ten features across a buyer and wonder why nothing landed. Nothing landed because they competed. Pick the one thing that wins the deal and put the whole spotlight on it.
Say ten things and they keep nothing. Say one.

4. "I just lost you for a second. Where'd your head go?"

Robbins reads where attention actually is, not where it's supposed to be. A drifted buyer keeps nodding, and that nod is walking politely toward a no. Name the drift, calm, no accusation. It snaps them back, and what pulled them away is usually the real objection introducing itself.

A nodding buyer isn't a listening buyer.

5. "Look at this one number with me."
Wherever Robbins points, eyes follow, and minds follow eyes. He directs the gaze first and works the second. Most reps let the buyer's focus wander onto the price while the proof sits unwatched. Point their attention at your strongest number right before the decision, out loud, and hold it there.
They weigh whatever you put in front of them.

6. "I'd rather have five real minutes than thirty distracted ones."

Robbins works in seconds of total attention, not minutes of partial. A distracted buyer gives you a soft yes to end the call, and it dissolves by morning. Claim the smaller, fuller window. It reads as confidence, it filters the tourists, and it protects the deal you think you closed.

A distracted yes never survives the night.

7. "Before you decide, put the phone face down for me."

Robbins controls hands and objects because objects steal focus. The deciding moment has to be clean of competition. You've earned the right to ask for it by then, so ask plainly. Buyers don't resent the frame. They respect the person who cared enough about their decision to protect it.

Own the last minute and you own the decision.
