1. "Before I say anything about the product, what made you take this call?"

Schein's rule: in a culture that worships telling, the one who asks first owns the room. Lead with the pitch and you're one more vendor talking. Lead with a real question and you're the only one who bothered to understand them.

Whoever asks the first real question owns the call.
<!-- image 2 -->

@jackblairofficial

2. "I could be wrong about what you need. Walk me through it."

Schein called it accessing your own ignorance. Certainty makes a buyer defensive, admitted uncertainty makes them open. Drop the all-knowing act and they stop guarding, because now they're teaching you instead of resisting.

Certainty makes them defend. Curiosity makes them talk.
<!-- image 3 -->

3. "You know your business better than I ever will. What am I missing?"

Telling puts you one-up, and Schein proved one-up quietly kills trust. Hand the expertise back. The buyer who feels like the smartest person in the room is the one who buys, because now it's their idea, not your push.

Let them be the expert and the deal becomes theirs.

4. "What have you already tried that didn't work?"
Reps rush to prescribe before they diagnose, the way a doctor who talks over you gets it wrong. Ask what failed first. You skip the pitch they've already rejected and land on the one thing they haven't heard.
Diagnose before you prescribe or you sell the wrong thing.
<!-- image 5 -->

5. "Say more about that."
Schein's smallest, strongest move. Most reps fill every silence with features. Don't. Three words that hand the floor back get you the sentence the buyer wasn't planning to say, which is usually the one that matters.
The next thing they say is worth more than your next line.
<!-- image 6 -->

6. "What would you need to hear to feel good about this?"
Instead of guessing which benefit lands, ask them to build the close for you. Schein's point was the answer is already in them. Pull it out and you're not selling anymore, you're confirming what they just told you they want.
Stop guessing the pitch. Ask them to write it.

7. "Where do you want to take this next?"

Schein called it process inquiry, letting the other person steer. Reps close by, letting the other person assign the next step. Flip it. Ask them to name it, because a step they choose out loud is one they take, not one they escape after the call.

A next step they pick beats one you assign.
