1. "I might be wrong about this, so tell me where."

In Give and Take, Adam Grant shows that people who talk with less certainty get more agreement, not less, because the listener stops defending and starts thinking. He calls it powerless communication, and it works hardest on a buyer who expects a pitch. Say it right after your first read of their situation, then stop talking. Whatever they correct is the real reason they took the call.

Certainty makes them defend. Doubt makes them talk.

2. "Can I get your advice on something?"

Grant's chapter on advice seeking: ask someone how they'd handle it and they take your side to answer, because they have to see it from where you're standing. On a call, ask the buyer how they'd roll this out if it were they start solving the objection before they've raised it, in their own words, which are the only words they'll believe later.

Ask for advice and they start telling it to themselves.

3. "You're probably right that now isn't the time."

The extraverts in Grant's 340 lost because they pushed through the stall. The ambiverts agreed with it, then asked a question. Say the sentence flat, like you mean it, then ask what would have to change for it to be the time. A buyer who was braced for pressure has nothing to push against, so they answer honestly, and the honest answer is your next call.

Agree with the no. Then ask what a yes needs.

The loud rep sells the pitch. The quiet rep
sells the buyer's words.

6. "Here's what I'd do if I were you, and I could be off."

Same principle, aimed at the close. Give the recommendation, then hand back the right to disagree. Grant's point is that the tentative version lands harder than the confident one when the listener is deciding whether to trust you, and every buyer is. Say what you'd do in their seat, name the one thing that would change your mind, and wait.

Tell them what to do like someone who'll still be there tomorrow.

7. "What would make this a bad decision?"

Ask it after the yes, before the signature.

Ambiverts in the study listened past the point where extraverts stopped, and this is where it pays. Whatever the buyer says is the objection that would have come back as a refund in three weeks. Answer it now, or agree it's real and let them go.

A yes that survived its own objections doesn't come back as a refund.

Now look at what Grant actually measured. Three months of calls, and the reps who won weren't the ones who sounded like winners.

Sound familiar?
