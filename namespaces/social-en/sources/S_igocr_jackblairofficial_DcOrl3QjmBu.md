THE 42 HOUR FUNERAL
In 2011 HBR published an audit of 2,241 US companies. Average response to a fresh web lead: 42 hours. Nearly a quarter never responded at all.
Read that as your competition's obituary and your opening. The bar in most markets is not sharp. It is absent.
You are not losing to better reps. Just faster ones.

ONE HOUR IS THE CLIFF
The same research team tracked 1.25 million leads.
Contact inside the first hour and you are about
seven times more likely to qualify the buyer than one
hour later.
Time boxes everything else you are proud of. The
best discovery script on earth loses to a mediocre
one that arrived 50 minutes sooner.
Speed is a skill. Schedule it like one.

BE THE FIRST VOICE, NOT THE BEST PITCH
The window exists because a fresh lead is still inside the moment of their problem. An hour later they are back in meetings and the itch is gone.
First contact sets the anchor every rival gets compared to. Arrive first and you are the standard. Arrive third and you are the comparison.
The first conversation frames all the others.

BUILD THE FIVE MINUTE MACHINE
Only 37 percent of audited companies answered
inside the first hour. The winners were not more
talented. They had wired the handoff so nothing
waited on a mood.
Systemize your speed: alerts on your phone, a two

SPEED WITHOUT A POINT IS JUST PANIC

Fast and empty still loses. The one hour call has one job: hold the moment. Confirm the trigger, book the real conversation behind.

Keep it under four minutes. You are not closing on this call. You are freezing the buyer's moment in place until the close can happen.

The first call's product is the second call.

AUDIT YOUR OWN 42 HOURS
Every pipeline has its own decay curve. Pull last month's leads and stamp two times on each: when it landed, when a human touched it. The gap is your leak.
Fix the top three leak sources before you buy one more lead. Volume poured into a slow funnel just buys more funerals.
Measure the gap. The gap is the territory.

THE LEADS WERE NEVER THE PROBLEM.
The stopwatch was.
HBR put numbers on what great closers feel in their gut: a buyer's yes has a half life measured in minutes, and almost every company burns it in the queue. Software can route a lead in a second. It cannot pick up on minute four, hear which problem is still hot in the buyer's voice, and freeze that moment until the real call. Reps who move at the speed of the itch inherit every slow room's pipeline.
