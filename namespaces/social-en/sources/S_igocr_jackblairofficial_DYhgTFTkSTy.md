1. "Test the headline before anything else."

From his 1932 book. The first rule he never broke.

He'd run two headlines against the same offer, same list, same week. Whichever pulled more orders won. The loser got killed. He was doing this in the 1920s, decades before anyone called it A/B testing.

He killed thousands of headlines this way. The ones that lived made BBDO clients millions.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. “Always promise a benefit in the first line.”

Rule he hammered in every junior copywriter meeting.

His piano ad opened with: “They laughed when I sat down at the piano. But when I started to play!” He never promised the lessons would work. He showed a scene where they already had.

The benefit was implied transformation. The reader filled in their own version.
<!-- image 3 -->

@jackblairofficial

3. "Use specific words. Avoid abstractions."

Headline rule #3 in Tested Advertising Methods.

"Saves you $200" beats "saves you money" every time. Vague claims sound like sales pitches. Specific numbers sound like reporting. Caples ran the tests. The data never budged.

He kept this rule for 60 years and never lost on it.
<!-- image 4 -->

4. “Say it in pictures. Words come second.”

From his junior copywriter training at BBDO.

He taught reps to describe a scene the reader could mentally see before making any claim. Then he closed. Most reps reverse the order and lose the prospect at the claim.

Paint the scene. The pitch lands in someone who’s already there.

5. “Make the headline as long as it needs to be.”

His running argument with the clever ad agencies of this era.

His most famous headline was 12 words: “They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano. But When I Started to Play!” He hated short, clever headlines that won awards but sold nothing.

Awards sit on shelves. His piano ad ran for 40 years.

6. "Inject news whenever possible."

From his headline rules.

"At last." "Finally." "Introducing." "New."
These words stopped readers cold in the
newspaper era. They still stop scrollers
cold today. Treat the news like the
prospect hasn't heard.

News interrupts. Pitches get skipped.

7. "Show easy results. Hide the work."

His pitch philosophy in one line.

His piano ad sold lessons by showing the outcome (the room listening in awe) instead of the process (months of scales). The hard work was real. He just didn't put it on the front of the envelope.

Lead with the destination. The road sells itself once they want to be there.
