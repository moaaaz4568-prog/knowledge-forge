1. "Besides you, who else weighs in on a call like this??"

Roger Dawson taught that a hidden higher authority kills more deals than price ever does. The phantom "boss" or "partner" shows up at the worst moment.

Surface every decision-maker before you present. If there's someone else in the room, you want them in the finish line, not ambushing you at the finish line.

Find the real decision-maker before you pitch.
<!-- image 2 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

2. "If I can make that work, are you ready to move today?"

Dawson's trade-off rule. Never give a concession without getting one back.

The second you drop price with nothing in return, you've taught them that pushing works, and they'll push again. Tie every give to a commitment, and the close instead of the discount buys you the more asking.

Never give a concession without taking one.
<!-- image 3 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

3. "Before we go further, I want to make sure this is even right for you."

Dawson's reluctant-seller stance.
Eagerness bleeds power. Reluctance makes the other side start chasing.

Signal that you're screening them, not evaluating you. The buyer flips from you to qualifying themselves to the whole frame of the call changes.

The reluctant seller sets the terms.

Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

4. "What number were you hoping to land on?"

Dawson's rule: never be the one who names the compromise. Whoever says a number first usually gives away room they didn't have to.

When they push on price, hand the question back. Let them anchor the one. Most of the time it's closer to yours than the panic in your head expected.

Let them name the number first.

Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

5. "When you say too much, what are you comparing it to?"

Dawson called the vague squeeze "you'll have to do better than that." Reps panic and discount against nothing.

Never drop price to a fog. Make them tell you what "too much" is measured against. Half the time there's no real number behind it, and the objection quietly dissolves.

Never discount a vague objection.

6. "While we're finalizing, what else would make this a clean yes for you?"

Dawson's nibble. A small ask at the decision moment feels trivial and gets agreed to almost automatically.

Used at the end, it surfaces the last little thing standing in the way, and clearing something small locks in the big yes right behind it.

The small ask at the end closes the big one.

7. "I really shouldn't do this, but for you I'll make it work if we lock it today."

Dawson proved a concession given too easily is worth nothing. One that feels earned and hard-won is what actually closes.

Never hand over the discount like it's free. Make it an exception you're stretching for, tied to a decision now. They feel like they won, and winners sign.

An easy concession is worthless. Make them earn it.

Read those back.

Not one of them is a hard pitch. Every single one quietly controls the moves of the call so the price takes care of itself. That's the entire skill. A master negotiator built a career proving deals aren't won by talking more, they're won by the person who stays calm and runs the sequence while everyone else reacts.

Software can fire off a discount code. It cannot sit across from a real buyer, feel the moment to hold and the moment to give, and run the whole thing without ever looking like it's selling.
