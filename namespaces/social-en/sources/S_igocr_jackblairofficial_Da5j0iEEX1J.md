1. "Let me slow down. This part matters."

Alex Pentland's MIT group hung sensor badges on negotiators and found the signals under the words, tempo, energy, emphasis, predicted outcomes within the first five minutes. Pace is the easiest signal to own. Slowing down at the money moment reads as control, and control is contagious.

Speed reads as nerves. Pace reads as power.

2. "Take your time with that. I'm not going anywhere."

In Pentland's data, calm consistency signaled status more reliably than any argument. Handing the buyer time does the same on a call. Urgency you cannot be used against you, and a rep who is not in a hurry reads as a rep with options.

Patience is a status signal.

3. "Can we take this to a call? Two minutes live beats ten emails."

The badges measured face-to-face signal, because that is the only place the channel exists. Tempo, tone and energy do not travel through text. Every deal parked in an email thread is a deal running on half its bandwidth. Move the hard conversations to where the signal is.

No one ever trusted an email more than a voice.

4. "Here's what I'm hoping happens today."

Pentland's group found early engagement and energy moved outcomes, and that openness invites matching. In the MIT salary study, when one side began mirroring the other, trust followed. Go first with intent. State plainly what you want from the hour and give the buyer something worth matching.

Energy leads, and trust follows it.

5. "Give me ten seconds with that number."

A deliberate pause is a consistency signal, and in Pentland's measurements consistency read as determination. Reps fill silence because silence feels like losing. It is the opposite. The seller who can stop talking in the middle of a negotiation broadcasts a certainty no script can fake.

Stillness reads as certainty.

6. "I don't need this deal. I want it."

The badges caught what buyers already sense: motivation leaks through the voice long before the words arrive. Need reads as pressure. Want reads as conviction. Say the true version out loud, and for once let the sentence match.

Want is a stronger signal than need.
