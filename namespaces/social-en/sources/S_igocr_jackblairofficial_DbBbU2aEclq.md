1. "I'm sorry you've had to chase me on this."

Alison Wood Brooks sent a man into a train station on a rainy day to ask 65 strangers to borrow their phone. The version that opened by apologizing for the rain got more yeses.

She calls it the superfluous apology. You apologize for something that was never your fault.

Open on the reschedule. The wait. The four emails you never answered.

You are not admitting fault. You are taking their side.

2. "Can I get your advice on something?"

Brooks ran this with Francesca Gino and Maurice Schweitzer. Everyone assumes asking for advice makes you look weak.

The data went the other way. The person you ask rates you as more competent, not less. And they start solving your problem with you.

Use it the moment their objection has you stuck.

Advice is the only ask that flatters the person you're asking.

3. "I'm excited about this one."

Brooks tested what to say before a high stakes moment. Karaoke singers. Public speakers. People doing math under pressure.

"Calm down" lost every time. "I am excited," said out loud, won.

Same racing heart. Different label. Your body cannot tell the two apart, so you get to name it.

Nerves and excitement are the same body. Only the word changes.

4. "And then what happened?"

Brooks studied 110 speed daters across two thousand conversations. The people who got the second date were not the interesting ones.

They were the ones who asked a follow-up. Not a new question. A question about the thing that was just said.

Most reps ask seven questions off a list and never once ask about the answer.

A follow-up is the only proof you were actually listening.

5. "I'll stop. What do you think?"
Brooks and Michael Yeomans named this
trap in 2025. Boomerasking.
You ask them a question, then answer it
yourself before they can. It feels like
curiosity. It lands as a monologue with a
question mark stuck on it.
The second you catch yourself answering
your own question, cut the sentence and
hand it back.
A question you answer yourself was never
a question.

6. "At this point the budget has a personality."

Levity is one of the four moves in Brooks's conversation model. Levity is not comedy.

It is a light touch on a heavy moment, and it does two things at once. It lifts your status and it tells the room you are not frightened of the problem.

Save it for the fourth delay. Never the first.

Fear is quiet. Nobody jokes about a deal they need.

7. "Can I take us sideways for a second?"
Brooks puts topic management first in
her model, ahead of the questions. The
best talkers move between topics more
often, and they say out loud that they are
moving.
Bad ones grind one topic until it dies, then
blame the prospect.
When the thread stalls, name the turn
and take it.
Say you're changing the subject and it
stops being rude.
