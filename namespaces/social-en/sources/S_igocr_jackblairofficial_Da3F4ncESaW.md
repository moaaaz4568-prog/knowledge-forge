1. "What's the part of this you'd normally never tell a salesperson?"

Edmondson found the best teams weren't cleaner, they were safer. People hid mistakes when honesty felt dangerous. Your buyer is doing the same on your call. Open a door for that makes the dangerous thing safe to say, and the real objection walks through it.

The objection they hide is the one that kills the deal.

2. "You're allowed to tell me this isn't a fit."

The teams that buried problems looked fine right up until something broke. A buyer nodding along isn't agreeing, they're managing you. Hand them explicit permission to push back and a polite maybe turns into an honest conversation you can actually close.

Silence isn't agreement. It's a problem you can't see yet.

3. "Let me tell you where we're not the best option."

Edmondson's safe teams had leaders who admitted their own mistakes first. So go first. Name your weakness before they find it. The buyer stops bracing for spin and starts telling the truth, because you just proved truth is safe here.

Admit one flaw and you make honesty the rule.

4. "What did the last person who sold you something like this get wrong?"
Suppressed problems don't vanish, they resurface later and cost more. Surface it now. Ask what burned them before and you find the exact fear quietly running this decision, the one they'd never volunteer on their own.
An old wound is steering the call you can't feel it.

5. "That's the most useful thing you've said. Tell me more."

In safe teams, the person who named the problem got thanked, not blamed.
Reward the hard truth out loud the second it lands. Punish it with a rebuttal and they'll never hand you another, and you'll close blind.

Reward the first hard truth or you'll never hear the second.

6. "What would have to be true for this to be an easy no?"

Edmondson's whole insight was that people need it to feel safe to say the negative thing. So ask for the no on purpose. It's lower stakes than the yes, so they answer honestly, and their easy-no reasons are your real objections, finally in the open.

Ask for the easy no and the real objections show up.

7. "Before we move on, what haven't I asked that I should have?"

The deadliest errors were the ones nobody spoke up about. Leave a clean open door right before the close. The thing they've been sitting on, the one that would've haunted you next week, comes out while you're still in the room to handle it.

The unspoken objection ghosts you. The spoken one closes.
