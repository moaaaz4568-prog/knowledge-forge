1. "I'm not sure if it's for you, but..."
Jones opens with a line that removes all pressure. The buyer's guard drops because you've handed them the exit.
People lean toward what they're free to refuse. Take away the push and they stop pushing back.
Lower the pressure and they lean in.
<!-- image 2 -->

2. "How open are you to..."

Instead of asking yes or no, Jones asks how open they are. Almost nobody admits to being closed-minded.

The question presupposes possibility. They answer the degree, not the decision, and the door is already open.

Ask the angle, not the yes.

3. "What do you know about…"

When a buyer thinks they know it all,
Jones lets them talk first, then fills the
gaps.

People believe what they conclude
themselves. Surfacing what they don't
know makes them want the rest.

Find the gap, then become the one who
fills it.

4. "Just imagine..."
Jones gets the buyer to picture life after
the yes before they ever commit to it.
The mind can't resist completing the
picture. Once they've lived it in their head,
not having it feels like a loss.
Make them feel the after, then sell it.

5. "Don't worry, most people feel that way at first."

When hesitation shows up, Jones normalizes it instead of fighting it.

A normal objection loses its power. The buyer stops defending it the moment it stops feeling like a dealbreaker.

Normalize the fear and it dissolves.

6. "What happens next is..."
Jones never asks "so what do you want to
do?" He tells them the next step like it's
already in motion.
Certainty is easier to follow than a blank
choice. You lead, they move, no awkward
decision moment.
Narrate the next step. Don't request it.

7. "The good news is..."
Whatever the objection, Jones reframes
straight into the upside without missing a
beat.
Every concern has a flip side. Pivoting to
the good news keeps the conversation
pointed at yes instead of stuck on the
doubt.
Answer every worry with the upside.
