1. "You don't have to believe me. Believe the people in your position who already did this."

Edward Bernays never made the claim himself. He put it in the mouth of someone the audience already trusted, a doctor, a neighbor, a name with nothing to sell. A claim from a neutral third party outweighs any pitch from the seller.

You route your value through a real customer and a real result instead of asserting it. Proof from someone with nothing to gain lands where your pitch can't.

The claim someone else makes for you beats any claim you make.

2. "Before we talk cost, let me reframe what you're actually buying here."

Bernays sold cigarettes as freedom and bacon as doctor's orders. He never argued a product on its own terms. He changed the category the buyer was judging it in.

You move the frame off the frame where you look expensive and onto the one where you're the obvious answer. The frame settles the verdict before the price ever comes up.

Win the category and the price stops mattering.

3. "Let me show you what you're really comparing this to, because it isn't the cheaper option."

Bernays controlled what the audience saw the product next to. Change the comparison and you change the decision.

You set the reference point yourself, out loud, instead of letting the buyer measure you against the cheapest thing on the market. Whatever you anchor them to is what they judge everything else from.

Whoever sets the comparison wins it.

4. "You said this is about budget. Can I tell you what I think it's actually about?"

Bernays researched the desire under the desire. People asked for one thing and wanted another, and he sold to the real want.

You look past the surface objection to the actual driver, the fear of a bad call, the pressure from mom above, the last vendor who burned them, and you speak to that. The stated reason is almost never the real one.

People state one reason and decide on another.

5. "Let me give you a reason to move on this that has nothing to do with me selling it."

Bernays never pushed the product. He created an occasion, a change in the world that made the product the natural response, and the pitch rode in on something that wasn't a pitch.

You attach the decision to a real trigger in the buyer's situation, a deadline, a change, a cost that just appeared. Now moving is a response to their world, not to your ask.

Give the yes a reason that isn't you.

6. "Most of what we covered doesn't matter. Which one of these is the real reason you'd say no?"

Bernays cut through the surface. People give many small reasons to hide the one that actually governs the decision, and only that one that matters.

You stop chasing every objection and make the buyer name the single one that decides it. Answer that, and the smaller ones fall on their own.

Kill the real objection and the small ones fall with it.

7. "If I do this right, it won't feel like I sold you anything. That's the point."

Bernays believed the most powerful persuasion is invisible. The moment it looks like selling, resistance climbs, so he removed every visible fingerprint of the pitch.

You strip the pressure, the closing lines, the performance, and just help the buyer see the situation clearly. The less it feels like a sale, the faster it closes.

The best close never feels like one.
