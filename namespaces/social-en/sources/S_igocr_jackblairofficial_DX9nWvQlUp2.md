1. The product didn't exist when the partnership got signed.

Itzler walked into a meeting with Richard Santulli, the CEO of NetJets, in the summer of 2000. NetJets had 500+ planes. Itzler had a pitch deck and an idea.

Six meetings later, Santulli granted him an exclusive to sell 25-hour cards on the NetJets fleet.

Itzler signed the deal first. Build the company around it second.

2. The first customer was a stranger at a Monterey coffee shop.

Itzler had no list. No leads. No pipeline. He was sitting at a coffee shop and struck up a conversation with the man at the next table.

The conversation drifted to private jets. Itzler mentioned the company. The stranger said, "I'm in the market for a card right now."

The stranger was Josh Kopelman, CEO of Half.com. Marquis Jet's first customer.

3. Three thousand handwritten letters a year.

Itzler wrote them himself. Personalized to each prospect. No templates. No mail merge.

8 letters a day, every day, for years. To Forbes 400 names, CEOs, athletes, musicians, the people he'd met once and remembered.

The reply rate was higher than any cold email campaign in history because every letter was a real letter from a real person.

4. The price was the filter.

$109,900 for 25 hours of flight time. Most prospects flinched. The ones who didn't became the customer base.

Itzler refused to discount. Refused payment plans. Refused tier-down options.

The buyers who paid the full number were the ones who flew enough to renew. Three years in, the renewal rate was 96%. Industry average was 60%.

5. The deal terms locked everyone in.

Marquis sold the cards. NetJets flew the planes. NetJets handled fleet, Marquis handled customers, both sides could have alone.

Itzler structured it as a sales funnel for Marquis customers who flew enough became fractional owners. NetJets fed off the Marquis pipeline.

The deal Itzler wrote was the deal that made the buyout inevitable nine years later.

The buyer wasn't a negotiation. It was a number Itzler picked.

The work was never about the money. It was about the discipline that built the money.
