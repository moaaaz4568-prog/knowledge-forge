THE WOLF OF WALL STREET RAN 1,000 BROKERS ON ONE SCRIPT AND SWEARE THE SALE WAS WON OR LOST IN THE FIRST FOUR SECONDS. MOST REPS SPEND THOSE SECONDS APOLOGIZING. HERE ARE THE 7 LINES THE SCRIPT RAN ON:

Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

1. "I'll be quick, I know you're busy, and I'm not calling to sell you anything today."

The Straight Line opens on four seconds. By Belfort's rule the buyer decides in that window whether you're sharp, enthusiastic, and an expert, and nothing you say later beats that first read. So the opener does one job: it takes the pressure off and hands you the floor. No apology, no "is this a bad time", no permission asked. Say why you're calling in one line, then ask a question before the whole decide to hang up.

The first four seconds are the line audition.

2. "Before I say a word, what would have to be true for this to be worth your time?"

Intelligence gathering. His system won't present until it knows three things: do they need it, can they pay for it, and can they decide. Ask before you pitch and the rest of the call is aimed instead of sprayed. Write down their words, not yours. Everything you say later gets built from this answer.

Aim first. A pitch without a target is noise.
<!-- image 3 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

3. "On a scale of one to ten, where are you on the idea itself? Not on me. The idea."

The Three Tens. Belfort's model says a buyer says yes when three things sit at a ten: the product, you, and the company behind you. Most reps sell the product and never check the other two. Ask for the number on each, one at a time. A six on the idea and a three on you is a different call from the reverse, and now you know which one to work.

Find the low ten. That's the whole objection.

4. "Does the idea make sense to you?"

The looping question. When an objection lands, the Straight Line doesn't answer it head on. It steps back to the one question that tests logical certainty, then resells the piece that came back short. "I need to think about it" almost never means thinking. It means one of the tens is low. Loop back, raise it, then ask again.

An objection is a smoke screen. Loop, don't argue.
<!-- image 5 -->
Jack Blair
@jackblairofficial

5. "Fair enough. Can I ask you one thing before you go?"

Half of the Straight Line is tonality, and this is the reasonable man tone: you agree with the buyer's right to hesitate, so the line stays open. The Stratton scripts used "fair enough" for exactly that reason. Deflect, agree, then come back in with a question. Never fight the objection's words. Agree with the person, not then move.

Agree with the person. Never with the objection.

6. "What happens if you do nothing about this for another six months?"

The pain threshold. Belfort's rule is that every buyer has an action threshold, the certainty they need before they act, and pain lowers it. His system gets the buyer to name the cost of standing still, in their own words, before any close. You don't invent the pain. You ask where it already hurts and let them describe it.

Pain lowers the price of yes.

7. "You're right to be careful. So let's make the first step small enough to be safe."
The Straight Line ends by shrinking the ask so the action threshold is easy to clear: a small commitment now, the bigger one after trust is earned. The brokers were trained to close on a small position first and grow it later. State the small step, then stop talking. Silence after the ask is part of the close.
Make the first yes small. The second one is easy.
