1. "What if it starts next month instead of today? Does the answer change?"

Richard Thaler won the Nobel Prize for proving people accept tomorrow what they refuse today. His Save More Tomorrow plan asked workers to commit future raises instead of current pay. Most signed. Their savings quadrupled.

When a buyer stalls, move the start date before you ever touch the price.

Today's no is usually just today talking.

2. "Staying where you are is also a decision. Should we price that one too?"

Thaler showed the status quo wins because it never gets invoiced. Doing nothing feels free, so it beats better options by hiding its cost.

Put the current state on the table as a purchase they re-make every month. Make it survive the same scrutiny your offer gets.

No decision is a decision. Price it.

3. "Most teams your size run the standard rollout. I'll set that up unless you tell me different."

Defaults move more people than arguments do. In Thaler's work, what people end up with is rarely what they chose. It's what was already ticking.

Stop handing buyers a blank page. Hand them a pre-built path they would have to opt out of.

People don't pick options. They accept defaults.

4. "Which budget does this live in?"

Thaler called it mental accounting. Money is not equal in the mind. The same dollar is sacred in one pocket and disposable in another.

A no from the operations budget can be a yes from the growth budget. Move the purchase to the pocket where it's cheap.

The price never moves. The pocket does.

5. "Every month this stays unfixed, you're paying for it. You're just not getting an invoice."

Behavioral economics measured it: losses weigh about twice as much as equivalent gains. Your buyer feels a bill they receive. They never feel a leak.

Total what the problem already costs them, put it in front of them, and go quiet.

Nobody moves to win. They move to stop losing.

6. "If you already had this and I offered to buy it back, would you sell?"

The endowment effect. In Thaler's experiments people demanded roughly double to give up a mug they had owned for minutes. Ownership changes the math instantly.

Flip it in their head. Once a buyer imagines owning the result, walking away starts to feel like losing it.

Ownership flips the burden of proof.

7. "Walk me through every step of this that feels like a chore. I'll delete what I can."

Thaler has a word for the paperwork that quietly kills good decisions: sludge. Most stalled deals aren't stuck on doubt. They're stuck on effort.

Audit the path to yes. Signatures, approvals, logins, forms. Carry every step the buyer doesn't have to.

Friction kills more deals than price does.
